# Let the library fund the generator

A solo founder should build a small, teacher-only generator that needs no login, runs on the cheapest model tier, and feeds a curated, human-reviewed resource library, because the library is the part that draws search traffic and carries ads while generation itself has become a free commodity. The generator alone is a weak business: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft now give verified US teachers premium AI at no cost, and every venture-backed teacher tool earns its money from district contracts that one person cannot sell. Ads can cover generation, but only narrowly: one generation costs roughly a tenth of a cent on a small model, while a tool page earns somewhere around $3 to $20 per 1,000 sessions, so the margin disappears on any mid-size or top model. Ads should therefore be the base layer, not the whole plan; the gentler money sits around the tool in the founder's own printable bundles, a cheap ad-free supporter membership, a sponsored newsletter, book affiliate links and inbound school invoices. On this report's rough estimates the mix yields about $90 to $560 a month at 10,000 monthly sessions, $1,200 to $6,500 at 100,000, and $12,000 or more at 1,000,000, the last being roughly Twinkl's estimated organic search traffic and not a planning assumption. The site stays legally light only if it remains adult-only, collects no student data and keeps ads off student-facing printables. Readers should weigh every number here knowing that the researchers could read only search-result summaries for almost all sources; the education ad rates, per-generation token counts and conversion rates are the weakest figures, and state student-privacy laws were not researched at all.

## Free generation is a commodity and districts pay the bills

The competitor set splits into three groups that earn money in three different ways, and none of the AI generators earns it from advertising. Venture-backed AI tools give teachers a free tier and sell quote-priced licences to schools and districts. Content libraries and marketplaces sell ready-made resources to individual teachers. Older free worksheet sites run ads and sell a cheap ad-free membership. The table summarises what the notes support; prices come from search snippets and third-party trackers that often disagree, so each should be rechecked on the vendor's live page.

Individual teacher plans cluster at **about $50 to $150 a year**, yet the real revenue comes from institutions. SchoolAI states outright that it offers no self-serve individual paid plan ([SchoolAI](https://schoolai.com/blog/understanding-the-new-free-plan)). MagicSchool, with nearly $63 million raised and roughly 8 million educator sign-ups, reportedly reached $10 million in annual recurring revenue within nine months of first charging by expanding to more than 6,000 schools ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/educator-built-edtech-startup-ai-magicschool-kahn/), [Valor](https://www.valorep.com/magicschool-company-profile)). Brisk raised about $20 million and SchoolAI about $32 million on the same free-teachers, paying-districts pattern ([Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/news/giving-teachers-their-time-back-our-investment-in-brisk-teaching), [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/press-release/20250401-schoolai-secures-25-million-to-help-teachers-and-schools-reach-every-stude/)). Money from individual teachers does exist, but it goes to content: Twinkl's parent reported turnover of **£98.9 million** for the year to April 2025, and TPT says more than seven million teachers have relied on it ([Wikipedia: Twinkl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twinkl), [TPT release](https://www.webull.com/news/15667269561369600)). Teachers pay for trusted, ready-to-use resources far more readily than for the act of generating.

The large AI companies have since removed "free AI for teachers" as a selling point. ChatGPT for Teachers is free through June 2027 for verified US K-12 educators with unlimited messages ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-for-teachers/)). Claude for Teachers, launched in July 2026, gives verified US K-12 educators a year of premium access at no charge ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/news/claude-for-teachers)). Gemini in Classroom is free to every educator with a Google Workspace for Education account, and Microsoft's Teach feature is free to Microsoft 365 Education customers ([Google](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/classroom-ai-features/), [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/education/blog/?p=10105)). These offers are US-only, require verification or district licences, and two of them are time-boxed. The teachers they cover least are those outside the US, homeschoolers, tutors, and anyone unwilling to verify, which is the audience an open, no-login tool reaches most easily.

Advertising is a proven model only on the static worksheet sites. K5 Learning claims over 20 million pageviews a month with two ad units per desktop page and one on mobile ([K5 Learning](https://www.k5learning.com/advertise)); Super Teacher Worksheets sells full access at $24.95 a year ([Super Teacher Worksheets](https://www.superteacherworksheets.com/about-memberships.html)); The Teacher's Corner once lived on ads alone and added an ad-free subscription ([The Teacher's Corner](https://www.theteacherscorner.net/about.php)). The researchers found no AI teacher generator funded mainly by display ads; the many free no-signup generators serve as lead magnets for paid products ([Flint K12](https://flintk12.com/tools/ai-worksheet-generator), [EssayGrader](https://www.essaygrader.ai/tools/worksheet-generator)). That absence is either open space or a warning, and the cost analysis below suggests it is a little of both.

The solo track record is sobering. Lesson Boost, an AI lesson-plan and worksheet generator, was listed for sale at about $30 of monthly recurring revenue; Porosheets shows $71 a month from four subscribers among about 200 users ([Flippa](https://flippa.com/12212224), [TrustMRR](https://trustmrr.com/startup/porosheets)). The nearest solo success is Questgen, an adjacent quiz generator at roughly $2,100 to $4,000 a month, which its founder credits to SEO landing pages for specific generator keywords ([Indie Hustle](https://indiehustle.beehiiv.com/p/his-solo-bootstrapped-a-i-side-project-is-making-2-100-monthly), [Starter Story](https://starterstory.com/stories/questgenai)). Small content sites do better: one Flippa-listed education content site earns about $50,000 profit a year from organic traffic and a 130,000-address email list ([Flippa](https://flippa.com/11633067-13-year-old-content-site-earning-50k-profit-year-with-organic-traffic-130k-email-list-and-evergreen-content-69-profit-margin)). All of these figures are self-reported marketplace or tracker listings, but they point the same way: a solo founder wins as a publisher with a generator attached, not as a software vendor.

## Ads pay for tenth-of-a-cent generations and little more

### No reliable ad rate exists for teacher sites

The researchers found **no dated, publisher-reported ad rate for any teacher site**. The only education-specific figures come from one low-quality blog that puts education content at about $5 to $7 per 1,000 pageviews on AdSense and at $24 to $38 on unnamed premium networks ([Organic Arbitrage, AdSense](https://organicarbitrage.pages.dev/articles/adsense-earnings-per-niche), [Organic Arbitrage, display](https://organicarbitrage.pages.dev/articles/display-ad-rpm-by-niche)). General estimates put Mediavine at $15 to $30 and Raptive at $20 to $40 per 1,000 sessions ([Hakaru](https://hakaru.io/tools/blog-ad-revenue-calculator)). The single tool-site data point is an Indie Hackers founder who earned about $87 from about 3,250 pageviews on AdSense, a rate near $14, at trivial scale ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-built-a-free-no-login-tools-site-and-it-quietly-made-90-with-adsense-this-month-842b010af3)). The researchers' planning range, which is a synthesis of these weak sources and not a measurement, is roughly $3 to $8 per 1,000 pageviews on AdSense and $12 to $30 per 1,000 sessions on a managed network before ad-blocking losses.

Ad blocking cuts into that range. A 2026 survey found 31% of US adults use an ad blocker, with use highest among college-educated, employed, higher-income people and households with children, which describes teachers ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416039/valuable-ad-blocker-consumers-earn-new-respect.html)). A 25% to 40% reduction on desktop is a reasonable assumption; nothing was found on how far school network filters block ads. Seasonality compounds this: Mediavine says ad rates drop in January and July and peak in the second and fourth quarters ([Mediavine](https://help.mediavine.com/why-does-rpm-drop-in-january-and-july)). A teacher site should expect July to combine the lowest rates with the lowest traffic, although no source quantified the summer traffic dip. This report therefore uses **$3 to $20 per 1,000 sessions, net of blocking**, as its working range.

The entry ladder to better-paying networks became much easier in 2026. AdSense has no traffic minimum. Journey by Mediavine now accepts sites with 1,000 monthly sessions from the US, Canada, UK and Australia ([Mediavine help](https://help.mediavine.com/what-does-it-take-to-get-approved-by-mediavine)). Raptive cut its minimum to 25,000 pageviews ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/raptive-drops-traffic-requirement-by-75-to-25000-views/558780/)). Ezoic moved the other way, to 250,000 monthly users for new publishers ([Ezoic](https://support.ezoic.com/kb/article/getting-started-ezoics-requirements)). Whether these networks accept app-style tool pages was not confirmed. Both major premium networks police AI content: Mediavine refuses "low-quality, mass-produced, unedited or undisclosed AI content", and Raptive rejected 13% of 2025 applications for AI-generated content ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/mediavine-terminates-publisher-accounts-over-ai-generated-content-concerns/), [Raptive](https://raptive.com/blog/enforcing-and-maintaining-raptives-quality-standards/)). An unreviewed AI library puts the ad account itself at risk.

### One generation costs about a tenth of a cent on a small model

Model prices span roughly 80 to 100 times between the cheapest and the top tier. Anthropic's pricing page, the one vendor page the researchers read directly, lists Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, Sonnet 5.5 at $2 and $10, and the top model at $8 and $40 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Third-party trackers put OpenAI's GPT-5 nano at $0.05 and $0.40 and Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite at $0.10 and $0.40; the official OpenAI and Google pages were unreachable and the trackers conflict on the newest model names ([Developers Digest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/models/gpt-5-nano), [CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/gemini-pricing/)). Only Google has a free API tier, limited to between 500 and 1,500 requests a day on its smallest model depending on the source, which suits a prototype and nothing larger ([aifreeapi](https://www.aifreeapi.com/en/posts/google-gemini-api-free-tier), [findskill.ai](https://findskill.ai/blog/gemini-api-pricing-guide/)).

No published token counts for lesson plans or worksheets were found, so the per-generation costs rest on the researchers' estimate of about 600 input and 1,500 output tokens for a typical item. On that estimate one generation costs **about $0.0006 to $0.001 on the cheapest models**, $0.016 on Sonnet 5.5, and $0.065 on the top Claude model. The number of generations per session is also unpublished; the researchers assumed three. A three-generation session then costs about a quarter of a cent on a small model, 5 cents on Sonnet 5.5 and 19 cents on the top model. Infrastructure is minor by comparison: free tiers plausibly carry 10,000 sessions, and a serverless stack costs roughly $5 to $60 a month from 100,000 to 1,000,000 sessions, on secondary pricing sources ([dev.to](https://dev.to/sungwoo_lee_e0f26be4a29fd/cloudflare-workers-pricing-5-minimum-10m-requests-included-16c9), [toolradar](https://toolradar.com/blog/supabase-pricing-2026)).

Put side by side, the two estimates give a narrow answer. A thousand sessions cost about $2 to $3 in model spend on the cheapest tier and earn $3 to $20 in ads. **Ads cover generation on a small model, with almost no margin at the low end of the ad range and a comfortable one on a managed network.** A Sonnet-class model would need about $50 per 1,000 sessions, which no source supports. The widely quoted ChatGPT loss figures, such as 36 cents of compute per conversation against under a penny of ad income, describe long conversations on large models and do not transfer to a single short structured generation ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/chatgpt-ads-ai-costs-business-model-neuron/)). No founder has published actual ad revenue against model spend for a free AI generator, so this break-even is arithmetic, not observed fact.

Reusing output shifts the balance further. A saved generation served as a static library page costs nothing in model spend and is itself an indexable, ad-bearing page, so every session the library satisfies improves the ratio. Dropdown inputs for grade, subject and standard raise cache hits and make the endpoint useless as a general chat proxy. That matters because an open generation route is a free model proxy the owner pays for ([vibe-eval](https://vibe-eval.com/fix/openai-bill-spike-api-abuse/)); attackers ran up $600,000 on stolen keys that had no spending limit in one documented case ([ITPro](https://www.itpro.com/security/cyber-attacks/hackers-ran-up-a-usd600-000-ai-bill-after-swiping-api-keys-says-metr-and-nobody-realized-for-weeks)). On a small model a million abusive generations costs about $800 instead of about $65,000, so the cheap model doubles as the main abuse control, backed by a provider spend limit, per-IP daily caps, an output-length cap and a global daily budget that drops the site to library-only when exhausted.

The more likely way to fail is too little traffic. One blog estimates that months six to twelve of a low-competition tool bring $50 to $300 a month, and that $10,000 a month at a $3 rate needs about 3.3 million pageviews ([Income Reality Check](https://income-reality-check.pages.dev/a/if-you-want-passive-income-paste-this-into-chatgpt/)).

## The gentler money sits around the tool

Every alternative below leaves the generator untouched. They differ mainly in how much audience they need before they matter.

**The founder's own digital bundles** are the line teacher-creators most often report as their top earner. One former teacher's blog, reported at about $8,000 a month across ads, affiliates, products and partnerships, earned most from a product line, and another teacher reported about $2,000 a month from digital products alone ([Side Hustle Nation repost](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/24731071-the-8k-month-blog-that-replaced-her-teaching-job), [second repost](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/25346443-2000-month-side-hustle-selling-digital-products-to-teachers)). Selling on the founder's own site avoids TPT's cut, where a Basic seller keeps 55% less a $0.30 fee, or about $2.45 on a $5 sale ([EduBracket](https://edubracket.com/articles/best-platforms-to-sell-teaching-resources-2026), [Designbeep](https://designbeep.com/?p=128020)). The products that fit are the ones a generator does not replace: polished full-unit packs, editable template sets and seasonal bundles, offered as a link beside free output. No data exists on how many free-tool users buy such bundles, and the modal TPT seller in a 2022 survey earned under $100 a month, so this line depends on product quality ([survey deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1HuOKA_4zLTT1FQSTbrAoBAgG9LoZb4FeHiDuOqRZqIQ/htmlpresent)).

**Gentle freemium** has the largest recurring potential and the clearest warning attached. The only edtech benchmark found puts free-to-paid conversion at 2.6%, the lowest of any software category ([Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/freemium-to-premium)). Reach Capital, which backs teacher tools, describes companies that gave a product to teachers free, added a paywall and "suffer as soon as they set up paywalls" ([Reach Capital](https://www.reachcapital.com/?p=5803)). The safe design declares at launch what is free forever and charges only for additions. ClassroomScreen keeps every widget free and charges for saving more, and the worksheet sites sell ad removal for about $24 a year ([ClassroomScreen listing](https://zoftwarehub.com/products/classroomscreen/pricing), [K5 membership](https://k5learning.com/blog/membership-program)). A supporter tier at roughly $30 to $50 a year for ad-free pages, unlimited saved history and bulk generation follows both precedents. No conversion data exists for ad removal on teacher sites.

**Newsletter sponsorship** pairs naturally with a tool that offers to email or save output. Education newsletters command about $10 to $18 per 1,000 subscribers per send, according to an aggregator that warns its own ranges are unaudited ([SenderCircle](https://sendercircle.com/guides/education-newsletter-advertising-rates)). At that rate a weekly email to 10,000 teachers earns roughly $400 to $700 a month with one sponsor per issue. Teacher media such as We Are Teachers and The Daily Draw, an individual creator's bulletin reaching over 47,000 teachers, sell exactly this, though none publishes prices ([We Are Teachers](https://www.weareteachers.com/media-kit/), [Art Projects for Kids](https://artprojectsforkids.org/sponsor)). Below about 10,000 subscribers the line is negligible.

**Affiliate links** are nearly free to add and nearly worthless until traffic is large. Teachers spent an average of $895 of their own money on supplies in 2024-25, so the purchases are real ([AdoptAClassroom](https://www.adoptaclassroom.org/?p=117782)). Amazon pays 4.5% on physical books and 3% on most categories teachers buy, while Bookshop.org pays 10% on books ([Amazon Associates](https://affiliate-program.amazon.com/welcome/compensation), [Bookshop.org](https://support.bookshop.org/en/support/solutions/articles/65000191390-can-you-explain-bookshop-org-s-affiliate-program-)). A "books for this lesson" block under generated output adds value, but one Indie Hackers builder saw zero affiliate conversions before switching to AdSense ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/launched-ai-tools-directory-with-8-600-seo-pages-2-months-in-9a311797f0)). No rates were found for Scholastic, Lakeshore or edtech tool programs.

**School plans** work only inbound. District sales take roughly 6 to 18 months and involve 6 to 10 stakeholders ([SaaS Hero](https://www.saashero.net/strategy/edtech-marketing-school-cycles-2026/), [CivicIQ](https://civiciq.com/blog/how-to-sell-to-school-districts-the-complete-b2g-guide-for-edtech-vendors-2026)). Even funded Sutori "really struggled" with district deals and focused on schools at about $2,000 ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/product/sutori/-MK9_192WzopzzOLhuXS)). The mechanism that works without a sales team is a teacher asking the principal: Chalk.com received a request for 72 licences within 24 hours of one teacher's recommendation ([SaaS Club](https://wp.saasclub.io/podcast/ryan-mckay-fleming-chalk-com)). A "get this for your school" page with an invoice flow, priced at a few hundred to about $2,000 and under local purchase-order thresholds, captures that demand at no cost to free users.

**Donations and grants** do not fund a site. Tips average about $5 and perhaps 0.5% to 2% of an audience ever gives, on weak sources ([VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/digital-tipping-creator-statistics-2026), [EarnifyHub](https://www.earnifyhub.com/blog/patreon-vs-kofi-vs-buy-me-a-coffee)). Wheel of Names, a free tool teachers use widely, runs on ads and not donations ([TeachersFirst](https://teachersfirst.org/single.cfm?id=21808)). Federal SBIR awards reach $305,000 for Phase I but are competitive research grants, not operating income ([NSF](https://nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/small-business-innovation-research-small-business-technology/nsf26-510/solicitation)). A two-sided seller marketplace is likewise too heavy for one person, since it demands moderation, payouts and copyright policing.

## Teachers want adaptation and accuracy, and they arrive through Google

About **60% of US teachers used AI for work in 2024-25 but only about 30% use it weekly**, which leaves a large pool of occasional users who have not settled on a tool ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly.aspx)). Adoption is uneven: 69% of high school teachers use generative AI against 42% of elementary teachers ([EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/news/2026-01-05-1-in-3-pre-k-teachers-uses-generative-ai-at-school)). Barriers are institutional more than technical. Only 19% of teachers say their school has an AI policy, and a fifth do not plan to use AI at all ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-60-of-teachers-used-ai-this-year-and-saved-up-to-6-hours-of-work-a-week/), [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/technology/whats-holding-educators-back-from-adopting-ai/2026/02)).

What teachers do with these tools is not quite what the founder's feature list assumes. Stanford's analysis of highly active MagicSchool users found the general chat assistant and the Text Rewriter, which re-levels and adapts existing text, far ahead of every other tool, each at double or more the usage of third place; elementary teachers use the rewriter more than chat ([Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/how-highly-active-k12-educators-are-using-ai-tools-like-magicschool)). About 8 in 10 AI-using teachers say it saves time on worksheets, assessments, quizzes or administrative work, and 64% see better quality in the modifications they make to student materials ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-60-of-teachers-used-ai-this-year-and-saved-up-to-6-hours-of-work-a-week/)). **Adapting existing material is at least as large a need as generating from scratch**, and a text re-leveller belongs in the first release alongside lesson plans, worksheets, quizzes and rubrics. No ranked data was found for rubrics, slides or parent emails specifically.

Complaints are thinly evidenced, because no Reddit thread or store review could be read and most comparison pages are written by competing tools. The recurring themes are generic output that needs heavy editing, features such as unlimited generations, output history and exports moved behind paid tiers, and Diffit passages too short for older students ([fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-tools-for-teachers-2026/), [Kuraplan](https://kuraplan.com/reviews/magicschool-ai-review), [Chalkie](https://chalkie.ai/en/blog/diffit-alternatives-teachers)). The best-sourced weakness is accuracy: an independent analysis found MagicSchool content matched grade-level expectations only about two-thirds of the time, weaker in science, and MagicSchool called the finding directionally correct ([MagicSchool](https://www.magicschool.ai/blog-posts/evaluate-grade-level-alignment)). Trust is also the marketplace's problem. Chalkbeat reported error-ridden "AI slop" on TPT in August 2026, and in October TPT announced plans to ban AI-generated resources ([Chalkbeat, August](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/08/03/ai-slop-on-teachers-curriculum-marketplace/), [Chalkbeat, October](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2026/10/15/teachers-pay-teachers-to-ban-ai-generated-teaching-resources/)). Reviewed, standards-coded, grade-accurate AI resources therefore lack a large home, which is the opening for a curated library. Claims that teachers are frustrated by print formatting, sign-up friction or missing exports are plausible but had no direct evidence.

Traffic starts at Google. More than 90% of teachers reported using Google to find lessons, and TPT and Pinterest are the main teacher-specific discovery sites, especially in elementary grades, though all of that survey data dates from 2017 to 2021 ([Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/edstat-according-american-teacher-panel-data-90-percent-teachers-reported-using-google-find-lessons/), [CITE Journal](https://citejournal.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/v19i4Math1.pdf)). The incumbents' search traffic leans on brand: roughly 44% of TPT's keyword traffic comes from brand terms and Twinkl's largest source is direct visits ([AirOps](https://www.airops.com/growth-strategies/teacherspayteachers-k9m2v7x4p1), [Semrush](https://cw.semrush.com/website/twinkl.com/overview)). A new site can reach only the non-brand long tail, meaning pages for a specific skill, grade and standard code, plus generator landing pages of the kind Questgen used.

That long tail carries a hard constraint. Google's scaled content abuse policy targets pages made in bulk without added value, whether or not AI wrote them ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/scaled-content-abuse/)). SEO blogs report that a March 2026 core update cost sites publishing AI pages at scale 50% to 80% of their organic traffic within two weeks; that claim comes from vendors with an interest in alarm and was not checked against Google's own pages ([Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/scaled-content-abuse-google-march-update-ai-pages-decimated)). The library must grow at the speed of human review. Beyond search, the documented pattern is founder outreach followed by word of mouth: MagicSchool's founder began by contacting teachers he knew, and FeedbackPanda grew by embedding in teacher Facebook groups ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/27/magicschool-thinks-ai-in-the-classroom-is-inevitable-so-its-aiming-to-help-teachers-and-students-use-it-properly), [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/arvid-kahl-how-this-indie-hacker-bootstrapped-feedbackpanda-to-55k-mrr-and-sold-it-in-just-2-years-06ad0a81bf)).

## Staying adult-only and student-data-free keeps the legal load light

The legal position depends on one design choice: the site serves adult teachers and never touches student data. The amended COPPA Rule, with general compliance required since April 22, 2026, now treats a "mixed audience" site as a kind of child-directed site and requires separate parental consent before a child's data goes to advertisers ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule), [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)). Whether a site is child-directed is judged on subject matter, visuals, animated characters and audience, and a terms-of-service ban on children does not settle it ([Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/federal-trade-commission-updates-streamlines-coppa-faqs/)). A teacher-only tool with adult copy and design most plausibly stays general-audience. The exposed part is the library: child-styled printable pages, or pages students are sent to, could be judged child-directed. The conservative design keeps ads off printable and student-facing views and places them only on the teacher-facing page around a resource. Google requires publishers to flag child-directed content for restricted ad treatment ([Google](https://support.google.com/platformspolicy/answer/3013851?hl=en)).

FERPA regulates schools and reaches vendors through agreements, so obligations begin when student records enter the tool ([Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/edtech-school-district-vendor-agreement/)). The widely used national data privacy agreement template bars targeted advertising to students and overrides the vendor's own terms ([SDPC](https://files.a4l.org/privacy/NDPA/NDPA_v2-2_STANDARD_WEB.pdf)). Features that process student work, such as essay feedback or drafting individualised education plans with real details, are the line between a consumer website and a vendor needing district approval. Demand on the far side of that line is real, since 57% of special education teachers used AI for such plans in 2024-25, but advocates raise privacy and legal concerns ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/teachers-are-using-ai-to-help-write-ieps-advocates-have-concerns/2025/10)). The founder should stay on the near side with de-identified goal banks and accommodation ideas, tell teachers not to enter student names, and store no more than an email address. Any school plan should be ad-free.

Two further points shape the product. The US Copyright Office holds that wholly AI-generated works are not copyrightable and that prompts alone do not make the user an author, so the library's protectable value lies in human selection, editing and arrangement, and competitors can copy raw generated pages ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/02/copyright-office-publishes-report)). And Google's rules for rewarded ads require the user to opt in to each one and forbid rewards with monetary value ([Google](https://support.google.com/admanager/answer/7496282)). A "watch an ad for extra generations today" option looks compatible, but that is the researchers' reading and not a ruling.

**The largest legal gap is state law.** California's SOPIPA, New York's Education Law 2-d and Illinois's SOPPA were not researched, and such laws can restrict targeted advertising by services used for K-12 school purposes even when the user is a teacher. No FTC statement on teacher-directed sites was retrieved either. The founder needs a lawyer's view on those two questions before relying on personalised ads.

## A lean publisher's mix, with numbers at three traffic levels

The recommended product has four parts. First, a focused set of generators (lesson plan, worksheet with answer key, quiz, rubric and text re-leveller) with dropdowns for grade, subject and standard, no login, and free print-ready and editable export done in the browser, which is precisely what incumbents hold back for paid tiers. Second, a curated library in which reviewed, standards-coded resources each get an indexable page, starting with elementary materials and longer secondary passages where the evidence shows weak coverage. Third, an optional free account that saves work and builds the email list. Fourth, cost discipline: the cheapest model tier, output caps, daily per-visitor limits generous enough that ordinary teachers never meet them, and a spend ceiling. The founder should publish a short "free forever" promise covering generation and export at launch, so that later paid extras take nothing away.

The monetization mix layers in as traffic grows. Light display ads on teacher-facing library and tool pages come first, starting on AdSense and moving to Journey at 1,000 sessions and Raptive at 25,000 pageviews, at roughly K5 Learning's load of two units per desktop page. The founder's own bundles and book affiliate links go in from the start because they cost nothing in user experience. A supporter membership at $30 to $50 a year follows once accounts exist, a single-sponsor weekly newsletter once the list approaches 10,000, and a school invoice page whenever a teacher first asks. A tip link is harmless and immaterial. The founder should not chase districts, build a seller marketplace, add student accounts or count on grants.

## Conclusion

The founder's instinct to keep everything free matches the market better than it first appears, because the large AI companies have already made free generation the baseline and no rival can charge for it for long. What the plan needs is a change of emphasis. The business is a publishing operation in which the generator manufactures raw material, the founder's editorial review turns it into trusted library pages, and those pages earn from ads, bundles and an email list. Review is the scarce input: it satisfies Google's content policy and the ad networks' AI rules, it supplies the only copyrightable layer, and it answers teachers' best-documented complaint about accuracy. One person's review capacity therefore sets the growth rate.

Three cheap tests would replace this report's weakest numbers with real ones within weeks. Running fifty real prompts and reading the token usage settles the cost per generation. A month of AdSense on a few dozen library pages yields the first true ad rate for this audience. Requesting media kits from two or three teacher newsletters prices sponsorship. Alongside those, a legal review of state student-privacy law should precede any dependence on personalised advertising, since that is the one unresearched question capable of changing the ad plan itself.

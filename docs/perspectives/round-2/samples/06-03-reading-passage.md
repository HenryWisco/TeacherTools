## Reading Passage

### Rebuilding a Nation

When the Civil War ended in 1865, the United States faced a question that would shape its future for generations: how should the former Confederate states be readmitted to the Union, and what rights should the four million people freed from slavery receive? The period that followed, known as Reconstruction, lasted roughly from 1865 to 1877. It produced major changes in federal law, brought Black Americans into public office for the first time, and ended in a political bargain that withdrew federal protection from Black southerners for nearly a century.

Three constitutional amendments formed the legal foundation of Reconstruction. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the nation except as punishment for a crime. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, granted citizenship to anyone born or naturalized in the United States and promised all citizens "equal protection of the laws." The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, stated that the right to vote could not be denied on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Together, these amendments were meant to turn freedom into citizenship.

The Freedmen's Bureau, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, was created by Congress in March 1865. It provided food, medical care, and schools for displaced people, and it helped freed families negotiate labor contracts. Its schools were among the first public education systems in the South open to Black children. By 1870, when the Bureau's work was winding down, Black southerners had founded thousands of churches, and literacy rates among Black adults had risen sharply from nearly zero at the war's start.

Political change was equally striking. Between 1869 and 1901, more than 2,000 Black men held public office in the South and nationally, serving in state legislatures, as sheriffs, and in the United States Congress. Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first Black member of the U.S. Senate in 1870. Such leaders worked to establish public schools, expand voting rights, and rebuild courts and infrastructure, though they often faced hostility from white opponents.

That hostility was organized and violent. The Ku Klux Klan, founded in Tennessee in 1865, spread across the South and used terror to keep Black citizens from voting and holding office. Congress responded with the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871, and the Klan Act of 1871 allowed federal intervention against conspiracies that denied civil rights. Federal troops and prosecutions reduced Klan activity for a time, but white resistance continued through other means, including intimidation, economic pressure, and violence.

Historians disagree about how to explain Reconstruction's outcome. Early twentieth-century writers, such as William Archibald Dunning and his students at Columbia University, portrayed the era as a corrupt and tragic mistake imposed on a defeated South. This "Dunning School" view shaped popular memory for decades. From the 1930s onward, scholars such as W. E. B. Du Bois, in his 1935 book *Black Reconstruction in America*, argued that Black Americans were active agents who built democratic institutions. Later historians have emphasized both the era's real achievements and the depth of white opposition, while debating how much the federal government might have done to protect Black rights.

In 1876, a disputed presidential election left the outcome uncertain between Rutherford B. Hayes and Samuel J. Tilden. A special commission and an informal understanding in early 1877, often called the Compromise of 1877, resolved the dispute in Hayes's favor. In return, federal troops were withdrawn from the last southern states still under Reconstruction governments. Within a few years, Democratic "Redeemer" governments had taken control across the South, and Jim Crow laws and disenfranchisement soon followed. Reconstruction's promises of equality would not be fully revived until the civil rights movement of the twentieth century.

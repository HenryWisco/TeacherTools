## Purpose and Guidance for Teachers

This discussion sheet supports a Grade 12 civics unit on three linked questions in US immigration policy: border enforcement, the levels of legal immigration, and the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) program. Students are not asked to reach a verdict. They are asked to understand each position at its strongest, analyze the tradeoffs, and evaluate how values and evidence shape conclusions.

**Neutrality guidelines:**
- Present each side in the terms its own supporters would use. Several positions are commonly described as "border security," "enforcement," "humanitarian," "rule of law," "compassion," or "economic growth," and students should be able to explain each one fairly.
- Avoid loaded labels. Use terms such as "undocumented immigrants" or "noncitizens without legal status" rather than "illegals." Students may hear other terms outside class, so it is useful to discuss why word choice matters.
- If students ask what the "right" answer is, redirect them: the purpose is to build the reasoning skills to evaluate policy, and reasonable people weigh the tradeoffs differently.
- Statistics should be checked against primary sources before class. Suggested sources are listed in the teacher notes at the end of this document. Verify current figures, since border apprehension numbers, legal permanent resident totals, and DACA enrollment change frequently.
- Keep the discussion focused on policy and evidence. If a discussion becomes personal, acknowledge the student's experience respectfully and return to the policy question. Students should never be asked to share their own or their family's immigration status.

**Suggested discussion routine: Think-Pair-Share**
1. Individual think time (2 minutes): students write a response to one question in the space provided.
2. Pair discussion (3 minutes): partners compare responses and identify one point of agreement and one point of disagreement.
3. Whole-class share (5 to 10 minutes): the teacher records key ideas on the board, grouped by position or by tradeoff.
4. Closing reflection (2 minutes): each student writes one sentence on how their thinking changed or stayed the same.

Students should be assigned to a neutral role in the first round (for example, "explain the strongest argument for a position you may not hold"). This builds the habit of stating a view fairly before critiquing it.

## Teacher Notes: Suggested Primary and Reliable Sources

Verify all figures against the most current release before class. Suggested sources include:
- US Customs and Border Protection, publicly posted border encounter statistics
- US Department of Homeland Security, Office of Immigration Statistics, Yearbook of Immigration Statistics and annual flow reports
- Congressional Research Service reports on immigration and on DACA (publicly available at congress.gov)
- US Citizenship and Immigration Services, pages on DACA and its current status
- Migration Policy Institute and the Pew Research Center, which publish analyses from different perspectives and are useful for comparing methods
- Supreme Court and federal appellate opinions in DACA litigation, available on the court's website

Encourage students to note the source, date, and methodology of any statistic they use in a discussion.

## Answer Key

These questions are open-ended and have no single correct answer. Use the following guidance to assess responses:

- **Understand:** Look for accurate descriptions of each position and of DACA's basic features and legal history. Responses should represent opposing views fairly, not as caricatures.
- **Analyze:** Look for specific tradeoffs, identification of factual versus value questions, and recognition that data has limits.
- **Connect and Evaluate:** Look for reasoned evaluation supported by evidence, acknowledgment of competing values, and clear criteria for judging sources.

A strong response to any question explains its reasoning, accounts for at least one counterargument, and does not rely on an unsupported claim.

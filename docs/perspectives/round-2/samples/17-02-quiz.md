**Passage: "Should Schools Start Later?"**

Every weekday morning, many middle school students in our town wake up before the sun rises. Supporters of early start times argue that the schedule teaches responsibility. Students learn to manage their time, get to bed at a reasonable hour, and adjust to the demands of the working world they will someday enter. Bus schedules are also simpler when schools start early, which saves the district money that can be spent on classroom supplies.

Sleep researchers see the problem differently. Adolescents' internal body clocks shift later during the teenage years, so many young people naturally feel alert in the late evening and drowsy in the early morning. When a school day begins at 7:15, a thirteen-year-old may be running on only six or seven hours of sleep, which is less than the eight to ten hours recommended for teens. Research on students who attend later-starting schools has linked the change to improved attendance and fewer car accidents among teen drivers.

Parents are divided as well. Some say a later start would leave them scrambling to arrange childcare before work. Others say they would gladly accept the change if it meant their children could focus in class. A school board member, however, has argued that the question should not be decided by how inconvenient the change would be for adults, but by what is best for students' health and learning.

The debate will continue at the next board meeting, where a vote on the proposal is scheduled.

## Answer Key

**Part 1: Multiple Choice** (1 point each, 4 points total)

1. **B** (1 point). The author emphasizes sleep research and student health throughout, so the position is opposed to early start times. Option A is wrong because the author clearly favors one side; option C reverses the position.

2. **D** (1 point). It states what a board member "argued" and includes judgment language about what the question "should not" be decided by. Options A and B are factual claims that could be checked, and C is a factual statement about the schedule.

3. **B** (1 point). The phrase signals that researchers frame the problem around biology and health. Option A is a misreading because the passage does not say the two groups disagree about the cause, and C and D are not supported by the text.

4. **A** (1 point). It sets two opposing claims against each other: the cost and simplicity benefits versus health risks. Options C and D describe facts that do not conflict in the same way.

**Part 2: Short Answer** (2 points each, 6 points total)

5. (2 points) Full credit for two accurate details, such as: early start times teach responsibility and time management; students learn to get to bed at a reasonable hour; bus schedules are simpler, saving the district money. One detail earns 1 point.

6. (2 points) The specific number of hours is concrete evidence that makes the claim about sleep deprivation measurable and persuasive, showing the problem is real for students. Full credit requires a clear explanation of how the detail supports the argument and the word "evidence" used appropriately. Partial credit (1 point) for identifying the detail without explaining its effect.

7. (2 points) The board member argues the decision should focus on what is best for students' health and learning, while the parents focus on the personal inconvenience of childcare. Full credit requires a clear contrast between a student-centered and an adult-convenience viewpoint.

**Part 3: Written Response** (3 points each, 9 points total)

8. (3 points) Look for a stated effect (such as encouraging the reader to keep following the issue or to form their own judgment), a clear link to the ending of the passage, and at least one supporting detail. 3 points: clear effect, specific evidence, well-explained. 2 points: effect stated with general support. 1 point: effect stated without evidence.

9. (3 points) Look for a viewpoint written in first person from a parent's perspective, a specific point from the passage that is agreed with or challenged, and a reason. 3 points: clear viewpoint, specific reference to the passage, reasoning given. 2 points: viewpoint and reference but limited reasoning. 1 point: viewpoint without reference to the passage.

10. (3 points) Accept either a claim that the presentation is fair or unfair, as long as it is supported. Strong responses cite evidence from more than one side (for example, both the supporters' benefits and the researchers' data), and discuss whether the author's stated preference affects trust. 3 points: a clear claim, two or more accurate pieces of evidence, and a thoughtful discussion of bias. 2 points: claim and some evidence with limited discussion. 1 point: claim without evidence.

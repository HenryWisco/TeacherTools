# Of Mice and Men, Chapter 1: Setting, Characters, and Foreshadowing

- **Grade:** 10
- **Subject:** English Language Arts
- **Class length:** 50 minutes
- **Class size:** 28, mixed ability

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, I can:

1. Describe the setting of Chapter 1 using specific details from the text.
2. Explain how Steinbeck introduces George and Lennie through what they say, do, and how others react to them.
3. Identify a passage that foreshadows later events and explain what it suggests.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| Setting | The time and place where a story happens |
| Characterization | How an author reveals who a character is |
| Foreshadowing | Clues that hint at what will happen later |
| Dialect | The way people in a particular place speak, including word choice and grammar |
| Mood | The feeling a passage creates in the reader |
| Symbol | An object or image that stands for a bigger idea |

## Materials

- Copies of *Of Mice and Men*, Chapter 1 (or the printed excerpt, if you use one)

## Lesson Sequence

### Step 1: Hook (5 minutes)

Write this on the board before students arrive:

> "Two men walk into a place where they are not welcome. One of them has a plan. The other has a habit that will get them in trouble."

Ask: "What do you think this story is about? What clues make you think that?"

Take three quick answers. Do not confirm or correct yet. Tell students they will test their predictions by the end of class.

### Step 2: Close Reading of the Opening Passage (10 minutes)

Students read the first two paragraphs of Chapter 1 silently. These describe the Salinas River, the wild land, and the bunkhouse. (Confirm the page or paragraph range with your edition before class.)

Pair students. Each pair works through these questions on their worksheet:

1. List three concrete details Steinbeck uses to describe the setting. Circle the words that appeal to the senses.
   ______________________________________________________________________

2. What time of day does the passage seem to take place? What words tell you this?
   ______________________________________________________________________

3. The narrator describes the river and the land with calm, steady sentences. What mood does this create? Why might an author begin a story this way?
   ______________________________________________________________________

Circulate and listen. Push pairs with questions such as: "What does the word 'wild' suggest about the land?" and "What would it feel like to stand there?"

### Step 3: Discovery Read: George and Lennie (12 minutes)

Students continue reading the section where George and Lennie first appear. Before reading, tell students: "As you read, track what George says and does, what Lennie says and does, and how George talks about Lennie."

Pairs complete this chart:

|---|---|---|

| | | |

After five minutes, pairs compare charts with a neighboring pair and add anything missing.

Then ask the class:

4. George warns Lennie several times about his behavior. What does this tell you about their relationship? Which line from the text best supports your answer?

5. Lennie repeats "George" and asks about the rabbits. What does Lennie's behavior reveal about his character that George does not say directly?

6. How does Steinbeck show that George and Lennie are different from each other, even though they are friends? Name one difference using evidence.

Take three to four answers. For each, ask: "Where do you see that in the text?"

### Step 4: Direct Instruction: Foreshadowing (6 minutes)

Use a question instead of a lecture. Ask: "When a movie shows a character looking at a locked door in the first scene, what do you expect to happen later?"

Take answers, then say: "That is foreshadowing. Writers plant clues early so that later events feel connected to what came before. Your job is to spot those clues."

Show this line from the chapter (paraphrase is acceptable if your edition's wording differs; check the exact text in class):

Ask: "What clue might this give us about what the story will involve? What future event could it be pointing toward?"

Allow a minute of partner talk, then take two answers.

### Step 5: Guided Practice: Find the Clues (7 minutes)

Give each pair two sticky notes. Ask them to search the chapter for one passage they think foreshadows later events. They write the quote on one note and their prediction on the other, then place the notes on a class chart labeled "Clues We Found."

Model one example first on the board: "Quote: [quote]. Prediction: [prediction]. Why I think so: [reason]."

Read a few notes aloud. Ask the class to discuss which predictions seem strongest.

### Step 6: Independent Practice (7 minutes)

Students write a short response on the back of their worksheet, using this prompt:

> Choose one character (George or Lennie). Using at least two pieces of evidence from Chapter 1, explain what the character's behavior suggests about the future of the story. Write 5 to 7 sentences.

Students who finish early may add a second paragraph that compares the mood of the opening setting with the mood of the George and Lennie scene.

### Step 7: Closure and Prediction Check (3 minutes)

Return to the hook. Ask: "Look back at your prediction from the start of class. Which clues in Chapter 1 support it? What surprised you?"

Students write one sentence on an exit slip: "One thing I predict will happen later in the novel is ____ because ____."

## Differentiation

**Support for students who need it:**
- Provide a vocabulary card with simplified definitions and a sample sentence for each term.
- Give sentence starters: "Steinbeck describes ____ using the word ____, which makes me feel ____."
- Pre-highlight the setting paragraphs and the George and Lennie passage so students can focus on the evidence.
- Allow students to answer orally or to dictate responses to a partner.
- Consider a brief audio read-aloud for the opening passage, if available.

**Extension for students ready for more:**
- Ask: "Steinbeck uses a dog and a rabbit in this chapter. Why might an author include animals? What might they symbolize?"
- Ask students to find a second foreshadowing clue and evaluate how strong it is compared to the first.
- Invite students to consider how the dialect of the characters shapes the reader's impression of them, and to find one example.

## Formative Assessment: Exit Slip (use during Step 7)

Ask each student to answer these two questions on a half sheet.

1. Name one specific detail that helps establish the setting. (Expected answer: any concrete detail such as the river, the wild land, the bunkhouse, the sycamores, or the dusk-like light. Students may name any accurate detail from the opening.)

2. Identify one piece of foreshadowing you noticed and what it might suggest. (Expected answer: a clear quote tied to a reasonable prediction, such as Lennie's habit of gripping things or George's warnings to Lennie suggesting trouble ahead.)

Sort exit slips into three piles: secure, partly secure, and needs reteaching. Use the piles to plan the next day's warm-up and small-group support.

## Answer Key

**Step 2, Questions 1 to 3 (sample responses):**
1. Details may include the river, the willows or sycamores, the wild land, and the bunkhouse. Sensory words may include "green," "still," or sounds and textures described in the passage.
2. The passage suggests late afternoon or evening, indicated by light and shadow descriptions in the text.
3. The calm, steady sentences create a peaceful, slow mood. Authors often start this way to draw readers in before introducing conflict.

**Step 3, Questions 4 to 6 (sample responses):**
4. George is protective and frustrated but caring. Accept any line showing his warnings to Lennie.
5. Lennie's repeated questions and eagerness suggest he is childlike and innocent, and that he depends on George.
6. George is practical and watchful, while Lennie is simple and dependent. Accept any evidence-supported difference.

**Step 4 (sample response):** The line points toward a possible future event involving trouble, danger, or a consequence. Accept reasonable predictions that connect to a clue in the text.

**Step 6 (sample response):** Accept any answer that uses two accurate pieces of evidence from Chapter 1 and makes a logical prediction. For example, Lennie's strength and his tendency to hold on tightly may suggest that his actions could cause harm later.

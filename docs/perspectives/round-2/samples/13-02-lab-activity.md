# Which Cup Keeps Water Hot Longest?

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Investigation Question

Which cup material (foam, paper, or metal) keeps hot water warm for the longest time, and why?

## Background

Thermal energy moves from warmer objects to cooler ones. Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through direct contact, and materials that conduct heat well (like metal) let energy escape quickly. Insulators (like foam and paper) slow heat transfer because they trap air, which conducts heat poorly.

Standard alignment: NGSS MS-PS3-3, Apply scientific principles to design, test, and refine a device that minimizes or maximizes thermal energy transfer.

## Materials (per group of 4)

- 4 cups, one each: foam, paper (paper cup with a lid), metal (aluminum or stainless steel tumbler), and one "control" cup (plain plastic)
- 1 thermometer (groups share; there are 8 thermometers for 7 to 8 groups, so use a rotation schedule)
- Hot tap water in a container (about 300 mL per cup)
- 1 timer or clock
- 1 cup of room-temperature water for rinsing
- Paper towels
- Pencil and data sheet

Teacher supply: 1 large insulated container of hot tap water per 2 groups, kept at a safe drinking temperature (no hotter than about 50 °C or 120 °F). Tap water from a hot faucet, or a thermos filled with water heated in a microwave-safe container by an adult, is acceptable. Do not heat water on a hot plate or with open flame.

## Safety Notes

- Use hot tap water only. Do not use boiling water. Teacher pours water from the container at the front of the room or fills cups at the sink.
- Keep metal cups away from edges of tables. Metal cups can get warm to the touch; handle by the rim or with a paper towel.
- Wipe up spills immediately to prevent slipping.
- Latex allergy: all materials in this lab are latex-free. Do not substitute rubber bands, latex gloves, or balloons. If using gloves, use nitrile or vinyl. Tell the student with the allergy ahead of time and confirm they are comfortable handling each material.
- Thermometers are glass or digital; handle carefully and return to the teacher's station.

## Procedure

1. Record your group's names and which cup each person will monitor in the data table.
2. Place one thermometer carefully into the foam cup (or check the thermometer reading at room temperature first and record it as the starting temperature).
3. Pour 300 mL of hot tap water into the foam cup. Put the lid on the cup if it has one.
4. Read the temperature immediately. Record it as Time 0 min.
5. Every 2 minutes for 20 minutes, read and record the temperature. Keep the thermometer in the water between readings when possible, and gently stir before each reading.
6. Repeat steps 2 to 5 with the paper cup, metal cup, and plastic control cup. Groups with fewer thermometers should rotate the thermometer among cups in a planned order, reading all cups at the same time points where possible. Teacher may choose to shorten the timing to 16 minutes if rotations take too long; record the change on the board so all groups match.
7. Rinse thermometers and cups with room-temperature water between trials, and return thermometers to the teacher's station.
8. Graph your group's results on the grid provided (time on the x-axis, temperature on the y-axis).

## Analysis Questions

1. Which cup lost the least temperature over 20 minutes? Use your data to support your answer.
2. Which cup lost temperature the fastest? How do you know from your graph?
3. Metal is a good conductor of heat. How does your data support or challenge the idea that conductors let thermal energy escape quickly?
4. Foam and paper trap air. Explain how trapped air slows heat transfer by conduction.
5. Why was a plastic control cup included in the investigation?
6. Name two possible sources of error in this experiment and explain how each could change your results.
7. If you were designing a cup to keep coffee warm for a long car ride, what material and features would you choose? Use evidence from this lab.

Write your answers in complete sentences on the back of this sheet.

## Answer Key

1. Foam cup (or paper cup, depending on data) typically loses the least temperature. Students should cite their specific numbers.
2. Metal cup typically loses temperature fastest. Graphs should show the steepest drop early in the trial.
3. Metal conducts heat well, so energy moves from the water through the cup walls and into the air or table quickly. If data show metal did not cool fastest, students should discuss measurement error or cup thickness.
4. Air is a poor conductor because its particles are spread far apart. Trapped air pockets in foam and paper slow the transfer of thermal energy from the water to the surroundings.
5. The control cup shows how the water cools without a special material, so students can compare each material against a baseline.
6. Sample answers: different starting water temperatures; thermometer readings taken at slightly different times; uneven stirring; lids not fitting the same way; room drafts.
7. Expected answer: foam or double-walled insulated container with a lid, because it slows conduction and reduces heat loss at the top.

Expected results (teacher notes): Typically, foam keeps water warmest, followed by paper, then the plastic control, with metal cooling fastest. Actual results may vary with cup thickness and lids. Teachers should check their results with a trial run before class.

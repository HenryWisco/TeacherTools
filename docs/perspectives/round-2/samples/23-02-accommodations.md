# Differentiation Ideas: Grade 5 Science, The Human Heart and Circulatory System

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

This sheet adapts one lesson: a 2-page textbook reading on the heart and circulatory system, a heart diagram to label, and six written questions. Every idea uses paper, pencils, the textbook, the shared projector, or things teachers already have. Nothing requires purchase.

## Students Who Need More Support

Students in this group are the blind student who reads braille, the Deaf student who uses an ASL interpreter, the two students with dyslexia, and the wheelchair user who cannot write by hand. Plan these supports before the lesson so they are ready when students arrive.

1. **Braille student:** Ask the school or district for a braille copy of the 2-page section. If none exists, the student's teacher of the visually impaired (if the school has one) can often braille a few pages. Prepare a tactile heart diagram by tracing the textbook diagram onto heavy paper with a pencil, then pressing over the lines with a ballpoint pen from the back so the lines rise. Use yarn glued to cardstock for the blood vessels if you have yarn. Give the student a copy of the six questions in braille or read them aloud before the lesson. Let the student answer orally or type on a braille writer or a basic keyboard if the school has one.

2. **Deaf student and interpreter:** Give the interpreter the lesson plan and the six questions the day before, so they can prepare signs for terms such as ventricle, atrium, valve, oxygen and vein. Seat the student where they can see the interpreter, the projector, and the teacher at the same time. Pause after each key point so the interpreter can keep up. Write key vocabulary on the board and leave it up during the whole lesson. Any video on the projector should have captions turned on; if it does not, use the text on the slides instead.

3. **Students with dyslexia (two students):** Read the 2-page section aloud as a class, or ask a strong reader to read it to a small group, before students read on their own. Print or copy the reading on paper with extra space between lines and in a sans-serif font such as Arial if you can, but a clear handwritten copy works too. Provide a one-page vocabulary list with the terms and a short plain-language definition of each. Allow them to answer orally, with a partner scribing, or by drawing and labeling.

4. **Wheelchair user with limited hand movement:** Arrange the desk so the student can reach the diagram without strain. Allow the student to label the diagram with a stick-on label or to point and say the label, with a partner or the teacher writing it down. For the six questions, allow oral answers recorded on a phone (if the student is allowed one) or answers dictated to a scribe. The student can also complete a labeling task by matching numbered cards to the diagram, which you can make from scrap paper in a few minutes.

## English Learners

Three newcomer students speak Arabic and have little English. Use Arabic where it helps, and use pictures and gestures for everything else.

1. **Bilingual word list:** Make a one-page list of 10 to 12 key terms (heart, blood, vein, artery, oxygen, valve, chamber, lungs, body, pump) with an Arabic translation written next to each. Check the translations with a bilingual staff member or a trusted online dictionary before printing. Keep the English term in the list so students learn it as well.

2. **Picture and gesture support:** Before reading, act out the path of blood with a hand or a fist: blood enters the right side, goes to the lungs, comes back to the left side, and goes out to the body. Draw a simple arrow diagram on the board. Students can copy the arrows and label them with words from the word list.

3. **Sentence frames for the six questions:** Give a frame for each answer, for example "The heart has ___ chambers." or "Blood goes to the ___ to pick up oxygen." Students can fill in one word or a short phrase rather than writing full sentences.

4. **Paired reading:** Pair each newcomer with a peer who speaks English well, and have them read a paragraph together. The peer reads aloud and points to the diagram while the newcomer follows.

## Students with Attention or Executive-Function Needs

The student with ADHD will benefit from these ideas, and several will help the whole class.

1. **Chunk the reading:** Divide the 2-page section into three parts. After each part, students stop and do a short task: label one part of the diagram, or answer one question. Mark the chunks with a pencil line on the copy so the boundaries are clear.

2. **Checklist card:** Write a short checklist on an index card or on the top of the worksheet: 1) Read part one. 2) Label the diagram. 3) Answer questions 1 and 2. 4) Read part two. Students check each step off as they go.

3. **Movement break:** Build in a one-minute stretch or a "show me" action after each chunk. For example, students put a hand on their chest and count heartbeats for 15 seconds, then multiply by four to find beats per minute. This is free, uses the content, and gives the body a break.

4. **Seat and timer:** Seat the student near the front and away from the window or door. Use the classroom clock or a free timer on the projector to show how long each chunk should take.

## Students Ready for More Challenge

1. **Pulse math:** Students count their resting heartbeats for 15 seconds, multiply by four to find beats per minute, then calculate how many times the heart beats in one hour (beats per minute times 60) and in one day (beats per minute times 1,440). Ask them to check whether their answer is reasonable.

2. **Trace the path:** Students write the complete path of one drop of blood, starting at the right atrium and ending back at the same place, naming every chamber, valve and blood vessel it passes through.

3. **Compare and explain:** Ask students to explain in two or three sentences why the left side of the heart has thicker walls than the right side, using the reading to support their answer.

4. **Design question:** Ask students to design a simple model of the circulatory system using materials on hand, such as tubes, cups and water, and to write what each part of the model represents.

## Task at Three Levels of Scaffolding

### Level 1: Full Support

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

Use the word list and the picture below. Read the section with a partner.

Label the heart diagram using these words: right atrium, left atrium, right ventricle, left ventricle, lungs, body.

Answer these questions. Use the sentence frames.

1. The heart has ___ chambers.
2. Blood goes to the ___ to pick up oxygen.
3. The ___ pumps blood to the body.
4. Blood that carries oxygen is called ___ blood.
5. Valves keep blood moving ___ in the heart.
6. The heart is a ___ that moves blood through the body.

### Level 2: Partial Support

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

Read the section. Use the word list if you need it.

Label the heart diagram with the six parts.

Answer the six questions in one or two complete sentences each.

1. How many chambers does the heart have?
2. Where does blood go to pick up oxygen?
3. Which chamber pumps blood to the body?
4. What kind of blood carries oxygen?
5. What is the job of a valve?
6. What is the main job of the heart?

### Level 3: Independent

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

Read the section. Label the heart diagram completely.

Answer the six questions in complete sentences.

1. How many chambers does the heart have, and what are they called?
2. Trace the path of blood from the body back to the body, including the lungs.
3. Why does the left ventricle have thicker walls than the right ventricle?
4. What is the job of a valve, and what would happen if a valve did not close?
5. Explain how the heart and lungs work together to deliver oxygen to cells.
6. Use the pulse activity to calculate beats per minute, and explain whether your result seems reasonable.

## Answer Key

**Level 1**
1. Four
2. Lungs
3. Left ventricle
4. Oxygen-rich (or oxygenated)
5. One way (or forward, in one direction)
6. Pump

**Level 2**
1. Four chambers: two atria and two ventricles.
2. The lungs.
3. The left ventricle.
4. Oxygen-rich (oxygenated) blood.
5. A valve keeps blood flowing in one direction and prevents it from flowing backward.
6. The heart pumps blood through the body.

**Level 3**
1. Four chambers: right atrium, left atrium, right ventricle, left ventricle.
2. Blood returns from the body to the right atrium, moves to the right ventricle, is pumped to the lungs, returns to the left atrium, moves to the left ventricle, and is pumped out to the body.
3. The left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body, so it needs more force and thicker muscular walls than the right ventricle, which pumps only to the nearby lungs.
4. A valve keeps blood moving in one direction. If a valve did not close fully, blood could leak backward, and the heart would have to work harder to move blood forward.
5. The lungs take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide. The heart pumps the oxygen-rich blood from the lungs to the body, where cells use the oxygen.
6. Students count pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by four. A typical resting heart rate for a 5th grader is roughly 70 to 100 beats per minute; accept any calculation that is correct from the count and a reasonable explanation.

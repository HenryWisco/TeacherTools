# Causes of the Civil War: Slavery, States' Rights, Economics, and Secession

**Grade:** 11
**Subject:** Social Studies (U.S. History)
**Class length:** 50 minutes

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, I can:

1. Explain how slavery, states' rights, economic differences, and political conflict each contributed to the secession crisis of 1860-1861.
2. Identify the stated reasons for secession in primary sources, including South Carolina's Declaration of the Causes of Secession.
3. Compare two historians' interpretations of the war's causes and explain how their evidence and emphasis differ.
4. Use a primary source to support a claim in a short written response.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Secession | The formal withdrawal of a state from the Union |
| States' rights | The argument that states hold powers that the federal government should not override |
| Sectionalism | Loyalty to one region's interests over the nation's interests |
| Tariff | A tax on imported goods |
| Abolitionism | The movement to end slavery |
| Interpretation | A historian's explanation of what caused an event, based on evidence and argument |
| Primary source | A document or object created during the time being studied |
| Secondary source | A later account or analysis that interprets primary sources |

## Background Timeline (for teacher reference)

- **1820:** Missouri Compromise sets a boundary (36°30′ latitude) for slavery in the Louisiana Purchase territory.
- **1850:** Compromise of 1850 includes a stronger Fugitive Slave Act.
- **1854:** Kansas-Nebraska Act lets settlers in those territories decide on slavery by popular sovereignty, leading to violence in "Bleeding Kansas."
- **1857:** Dred Scott v. Sandford: the Supreme Court rules that Black Americans are not citizens and that Congress cannot ban slavery in the territories.
- **1859:** John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry.
- **1860:** Abraham Lincoln is elected president without winning a single Southern state's electoral votes. South Carolina secedes in December 1860.
- **1861:** Six more states secede by February; Fort Sumter is attacked on April 12, 1861.

## Lesson Sequence (50 minutes)

### Step 1: Hook (5 minutes)

Write this question on the board: **"Why do people leave a group they belong to?"**

Have students jot two or three answers on a sticky note or scrap paper for 90 seconds, then take three quick answers aloud. Connect: "Today we study why eleven Southern states left the United States in 1860-1861, and what they said about their reasons."

### Step 2: Direct Instruction (10 minutes)

Present the four causes using the board. Keep notes brief.

1. **Slavery:** Slavery was the central issue of the sectional conflict. Southern leaders defended it as essential to their economy and society. Northern opposition ranged from anti-expansion positions to abolitionism. The Dred Scott decision and Lincoln's 1860 election intensified the conflict.
2. **States' rights:** Southern leaders argued that states could judge federal laws and leave the Union if the federal government threatened their interests. Northern leaders generally argued that the Constitution created a permanent Union and that national law should prevail.
3. **Economics:** The South relied on cotton exports and enslaved labor. The North had a growing industrial and wage-labor economy. Disputes over tariffs, banking, and infrastructure deepened the divide.
4. **Secession:** Southern states argued that the election of Lincoln, a Republican opposed to slavery's expansion, threatened their rights. Each state issued a declaration explaining its decision.

Tell students: "Historians disagree about which cause mattered most. Today you will read the states' own words, then compare how historians weigh the evidence."

### Step 3: Guided Practice: Primary Source Analysis (15 minutes)

Distribute **Source Set A**. Read the first excerpt aloud together. Model the analysis process by asking the questions below and writing answers on the board.

**Source Set A: Primary Sources (excerpts)**

**Source A1: South Carolina, Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union (December 24, 1860)**

> "The Constitution of the United States, in its fourth Article, provides as follows: 'No person held to service or labor in one State, under the laws thereof, escaping into another, shall, in consequence of any law or regulation therein, be discharged from such service or labor, but shall be delivered up, on claim of the party to whom such service or labor may be due.' This stipulation was so material to the compact that without it that compact would not have been entered into by the Southern States... A geographical line has been drawn across the United States, as a line of separation, the citizens of the one section to be the exclusive advocates of negro equality... and the people of the other section, the owners of slaves, have been denounced as the sinners... They have destroyed the Compact."

*Note for teacher: The full declaration is available from the Avalon Project at Yale Law School and from the Library of Congress. The excerpt above is brief and reflects the document's language. Verify wording against your preferred edition before printing.*

**Source A2: Mississippi, A Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of the State of Mississippi from the Federal Union (January 9, 1861)**

> "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery, the greatest material interest of the world. Its labor supplies the product which constitutes by far the largest and most important portions of commerce of the earth... We, too, have heard the sound of the approaching conflict in the blast of the drum... Argument is exhausted... Our decision is made, and we have only to fear that the ensuing calamity will not be as severe as we know it will be."

*Note for teacher: Verify wording against a reliable edition before printing.*

**Source A3: Abraham Lincoln, First Inaugural Address (March 4, 1861)**

> "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so... Physically speaking, we cannot separate. We cannot remove our respective sections from each other, nor build an impassable wall between them... In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war."

**Guided questions (ask the class; expected answers in parentheses):**

1. What does South Carolina say the Constitution requires about enslaved people who escape? (It requires that they be returned to their owners. The declaration says Northern states failed to honor this.)
2. According to Mississippi, what makes the South's position distinct? (Its identification with slavery and the product it supplies to world commerce.)
3. What does Lincoln promise about slavery where it already exists? (He says he has no purpose or lawful right to interfere with it in the states where it exists.)
4. Which source gives the most direct reason for secession? (Students should identify South Carolina's and Mississippi's explicit defense of slavery. Lincoln's address, by contrast, argues for the permanence of the Union.)

Point out: Lincoln's address does not promise to end slavery. Students should notice that the sources reflect different political goals.

### Step 4: Independent Practice: Historians' Interpretations (12 minutes)

Pairs read **Source Set B**, then complete the comparison chart.

**Source Set B: Historians' Interpretations (paraphrased)**

**B1: Historian "Eric Foner" (Ph.D. Columbia; wrote *Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men* and *The Fiery Trial*)**
Foner argues that the conflict over slavery's expansion into the territories, and the Republican Party's commitment to "free soil" and "free labor," was central to the sectional crisis. He emphasizes that Southern leaders saw Republican politics as a direct threat to slavery's future.

**B2: Historian "Charles Beard" (early 20th century; *The Rise of American Civilization*, 1927)**
Beard argued that economic conflicts between an industrializing North and an agrarian South, including tariffs and banking, were a major driver of the war. His interpretation has been widely challenged, in part because later historians showed that slavery was closely tied to the political and economic conflicts he described.

**B3: Historian "James McPherson" (*Battle Cry of Freedom*, 1988)**
McPherson argues that slavery was the central cause of the war, because the conflict over its expansion and the Southern commitment to it made compromise impossible. He also notes that Southern leaders said so themselves in their secession documents.

*Teacher note: These are paraphrases of well-known scholarship. Before class, confirm the wording and titles against the historians' books or a reputable encyclopedia, and adjust if your edition differs.*

**Comparison Chart (complete with your partner):**

| Historian | Main cause emphasized | Evidence used | One thing this interpretation leaves out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foner | | | |
| Beard | | | |
| McPherson | | | |

**Expected answers (for teacher checking):**
- Foner: Main cause is slavery's expansion and the threat to it. Evidence: Republican platform, political debates, free labor ideology. Leaves out: Economic disputes as a separate driver, though he does not deny them.
- Beard: Main cause is economic conflict. Evidence: tariffs, banking, industrial versus agrarian economies. Leaves out: Slavery as the foundation of the Southern economy, which later historians stressed.
- McPherson: Main cause is slavery. Evidence: Secession declarations and the impossibility of compromise. Leaves out: Students may note that he gives less weight to purely economic or constitutional factors, though he does not ignore them.

### Step 5: Closure and Exit Ticket (8 minutes)

Discuss briefly: "Which historian's argument do you find most convincing, and why? What would make you change your mind?" Take two or three responses. Then have students complete the exit ticket.

## Differentiation

**For students who need support:**
- Provide a glossary card with the vocabulary terms and a simplified version of each source (one-sentence summary per excerpt).
- Give sentence starters: "South Carolina claims that ___ because ___." "The historian ___ believes ___ because ___."
- Pair with a strong reader for Source Set B.

**For students ready for more:**
- Ask them to compare the South Carolina and Mississippi declarations. What do they emphasize differently?
- Have them read the full text of Lincoln's First Inaugural Address and identify two places where he tries to reassure the South.
- Challenge them to find a historian who argues that states' rights, not slavery, was the primary cause, and evaluate that argument against the evidence.

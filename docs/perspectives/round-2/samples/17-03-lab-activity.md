# Lab: Does Mass Stay the Same in a Chemical Reaction?

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

**Group Members:** [Names]

## Investigation Question

Does the total mass of the materials change when a chemical reaction takes place in a closed container?

## Background

In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged to form new substances, but the atoms themselves are not created or destroyed. Because of this, the total mass of all the substances before the reaction equals the total mass after the reaction, as long as no gas or material escapes from the container. This idea is called the Law of Conservation of Mass, and it is one of the core ideas scientists use to explain chemical change.

In this lab, you will mix baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) with vinegar (dilute acetic acid) inside a sealed zip-top bag. The reaction produces carbon dioxide gas, and the bag is designed to keep that gas inside so you can measure the total mass before and after.

## Materials (per group)

- 1 digital balance that reads to 0.01 g (or 0.1 g)
- 1 quart-size zip-top plastic bag (heavy-duty, with a tight seal)
- 1 small plastic cup or paper cup (about 4 oz)
- 1 plastic spoon or measuring spoon (1 teaspoon)
- 2 tablespoons baking soda
- 1/4 cup white vinegar (5% acidity)
- 1 small piece of paper towel
- 1 pencil
- 1 copy of this lab sheet
- Tape or a marker for labeling the bag
- Paper towels for cleanup

## Safety Notes

- Wear safety goggles at all times during the lab.
- Vinegar and baking soda are mild, but avoid getting either in your eyes. If contact happens, rinse with water and tell your teacher.
- Do not taste or smell materials directly. Waft gently if you need to smell something.
- Keep the zip-top bag closed as much as possible. Do not squeeze the bag hard while it is reacting, because it may burst.
- Wipe up spills right away to prevent slipping.
- **Teacher/adult step:** The teacher should check that all bags are sealed correctly before students begin and should handle the balance setup and any cleanup of large spills.
- Wash hands after the lab.

## Procedure

1. Put on your safety goggles. Get all materials from your teacher and place them on the lab table.
2. Label your zip-top bag with your group name.
3. Pour 1/4 cup of vinegar into the small cup. Place the cup inside the zip-top bag, standing upright and being careful that the cup does not tip.
4. Fold the paper towel and place 2 tablespoons of baking soda on it. Wrap the paper towel loosely around the baking soda so it does not mix with the vinegar yet. Place the paper towel packet inside the bag, on top of the flat part of the bag and away from the cup.
5. Carefully press as much air out of the bag as you can, then seal it completely. Check that the seal is tight all the way across.
6. Place the sealed bag on the balance. Wait until the reading stops changing. Record this as the **mass before the reaction** in Data Table 1.
7. Remove the bag from the balance. Holding the bag flat over the table, gently tip it so the paper towel packet falls into the vinegar cup. Do this slowly and keep the bag closed.
8. Immediately lay the bag flat on the table. Gently press the bag once to release any gas that builds up, keeping the seal closed. Observe the reaction for 2 minutes. Record your observations in Data Table 2.
9. When the bubbling has stopped, place the sealed bag back on the balance. Wait until the reading stops changing. Record this as the **mass after the reaction** in Data Table 1.
10. Calculate the change in mass by subtracting the mass before from the mass after. Record it in Data Table 1.
11. Open the bag over the sink or trash can, dispose of the contents as your teacher directs, and wipe the table clean.

## Teacher Notes

**Purpose and alignment:** This lab supports NGSS MS-PS1-7, which asks students to develop and use a model to describe that atoms are conserved in a chemical reaction and that the total mass is the same before and after. Students gather evidence that mass is conserved in a closed system.

**Preparation:** Test the procedure once before class. Use a quart zip-top bag with a strong seal. Use fresh baking soda, since old baking soda may react less. Pre-measure vinegar and baking soda for each group to save time.

**Expected results:** The reaction produces carbon dioxide gas, which causes bubbling and fizzing. If the bag stays sealed, the change in mass should be very close to zero, typically within about 0.01 to 0.05 g with a balance reading to 0.01 g. Small differences are normal due to balance precision, slight gas leakage, moisture, or bag handling. Students should understand that a small change does not disprove conservation but reflects measurement limits.

**Common issues:** If the bag leaks, the mass will drop noticeably after the reaction. Use this as a teaching moment about the importance of a closed system. If the bag puffs up a lot, the gas may stretch the seal; a larger bag or less baking soda can help.

**Answer Key**

1. Evidence includes bubbling or fizzing, a rise in temperature (sometimes slight), the bag inflating, and the formation of a gas. Any two observations are acceptable.
2. Answers will vary by group. The total mass before the reaction should match the total mass after the reaction within measurement error.
3. Expected answer: the change should be very small, close to 0 g, usually within about 0.05 g. A change of this size reflects measurement error rather than a true change in mass.
4. Possible reasons include a small leak in the seal, moisture on the bag or cup, air pressure changes, balance drift, or inconsistent timing when weighing.
5. Carbon dioxide gas. The sealed bag keeps the gas inside the system, so any gas formed is still included in the total mass measured.
6. Expected answer: In a closed system, atoms are rearranged during the reaction but none are created or destroyed. Therefore, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products, including any gas produced.
7. The measured mass would decrease, because the carbon dioxide gas leaving the bag takes mass out of the closed system. This would show that the mass appears to change only because matter escaped, not because mass was lost by the reaction itself.

# Quiz: How a Local School District Budget Works

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________  **Score:** _______ / 30

Directions: Answer all 10 questions. Questions 1 to 5 are multiple choice (3 points each). Questions 6 to 10 are short answer or reasoning (3 points each). Show your reasoning where asked.

## Part A: Multiple Choice

1. A homeowner's property tax bill is calculated mainly from which two values?
A. The home's assessed value and the local tax rate
B. The homeowner's income and the number of children in the household
C. The state's total education budget and the number of school board members
D. The home's purchase price and the year it was built

2. A school district has a total assessed property value of $800 million. The local school tax rate is 2.5 mills per dollar of assessed value (1 mill = $0.001). How much revenue does the district raise from this rate?
A. $200,000
B. $2,000,000
C. $20,000,000
D. $200,000,000

3. Which statement best describes the role of the local school board?
A. It sets the state's education standards and approves state tests.
B. It adopts the district budget, sets policy, and hires and oversees the superintendent.
C. It collects property taxes directly from homeowners.
D. It approves individual teachers' lesson plans each semester.

4. Which of the following is an example of operating spending rather than capital spending?
A. Building a new gymnasium
B. Replacing the roof of an existing school
C. Paying teachers' salaries for the school year
D. Buying a new bus fleet that will last 12 years

5. A district wants to build a new elementary school that will cost $30 million and last 40 years. Which funding method is most commonly used for this type of project?
A. Using money from the general operating fund in a single year
B. Issuing voter-approved bonds that are repaid over many years
C. Asking each student's family for a fee at enrollment
D. Taking a short-term loan that must be repaid within 30 days

## Part B: Short Answer and Reasoning

6. Define "state aid" in one or two sentences. Then explain why a district with a low property tax base might receive a larger share of its budget from the state.

7. Explain the difference between a bond and a levy. For each, state whether it usually requires voter approval and what it is typically used to pay for.

8. A district's enrollment drops by 10 percent over three years, but its operating costs for buildings stay almost the same. Explain two ways this could strain the operating budget.

9. Application: A town reassesses all homes, and average assessed values rise by 20 percent. The school board keeps the tax rate the same. Will total property tax revenue for the district go up, go down, or stay the same? Explain your answer in two or three sentences.

10. Reasoning: Some people argue that capital projects should be paid for out of the operating budget so that no debt is created. Others argue bonds are the better choice. Give one strength of each position, and state which you think is stronger for a 40-year building, with a reason.

## Answer Key

Point values: each question is worth 3 points (30 total). Full credit for multiple choice requires the correct letter. For short answer, award 3 for a complete, accurate answer, 2 for mostly accurate with one gap, 1 for partial understanding, 0 for missing or incorrect.

1. **A** (3 points). Property tax is generally assessed value multiplied by the tax rate; income and family size are not factors.

2. **B** (3 points). $800,000,000 × 2.5 / 1,000 = $2,000,000.

3. **B** (3 points). School boards adopt the budget, set policy, and hire and oversee the superintendent; they do not collect taxes or set state standards.

4. **C** (3 points). Operating spending covers recurring costs like salaries and supplies. The other options are one-time or long-lived improvements, which are capital spending.

5. **B** (3 points). Voter-approved bonds spread the cost of long-lived buildings over the years they are used.

6. (3 points). Sample answer: State aid is money the state government gives to local school districts, usually from state taxes. Districts with a low property tax base receive a larger share because the state uses funding formulas to equalize resources, so poorer districts with less local property wealth get more state support.

7. (3 points). Sample answer: A bond is a long-term borrowing instrument, usually approved by voters, used to pay for capital projects such as buildings and major renovations. A levy is a tax, approved by voters in many states, used to fund operating costs such as salaries, or sometimes temporary needs; its approval and duration vary by state. Accept answers that note state-specific rules.

8. (3 points). Sample answer: First, with fewer students, per-pupil state and local funding may fall while fixed building costs (heat, maintenance, custodial staff) remain, squeezing the budget. Second, the district may need to cut programs or staff or consolidate schools, and it may still carry costs for buildings it no longer fully uses. Accept any two well-reasoned effects.

9. (3 points). Revenue goes up. Total revenue equals assessed value times the tax rate; if assessed values rise 20 percent and the rate is unchanged, revenue rises about 20 percent (before any exemptions or appeals). Students should note that individual bills may not all rise by the same amount if reassessment changes homes unevenly, but total revenue rises.

10. (3 points). Sample answer: Paying from the operating budget avoids interest costs and debt, and keeps the district from tying up future taxpayers. Bonds spread a large cost across the years the building is used, so current operating programs are not crowded out. For a 40-year building, bonds are generally the stronger choice because the people who use the building over four decades share its cost, and a one-year operating budget cannot absorb a $30 million expense without cutting programs. Full credit requires a stated position with a reason.

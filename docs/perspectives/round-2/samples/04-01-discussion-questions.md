# Hamlet, Act 3: Socratic Seminar Discussion Questions

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Seminar Overview

These ten questions are for an AP English Literature Socratic seminar on Act 3 of Shakespeare's *Hamlet*. Each question quotes a specific line, identifies the speaker, and gives the act, scene, and line number. Students should have the whole act read before the seminar.

Line numbers follow the standard Folger-style through-numbering used in most modern editions. If your edition numbers differently, check the citation against your own text before printing.

## Suggested Routine

Use this sequence for a 50-minute seminar:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Individual silent reading of the three question groups. Students mark one question they want to argue. |
| 5–12 min | Think-pair-share: each pair drafts one claim and one piece of textual evidence for their marked question. |
| 12–40 min | Seminar discussion. Teacher asks a starter question; students respond to each other, not only to the teacher. |
| 40–48 min | Students write a two-sentence claim about the strongest point raised. |
| 48–50 min | Teacher closes by naming one tension the class did not resolve. |

Track participation with tally marks: one mark for each substantive contribution that builds on a peer's point.

## Understand

Focus: what the language says and what the characters do with it.

1. Hamlet tells Ophelia, "Get thee to a nunnery. Why wouldst thou be a breeder of sinners?" (Act 3, Scene 1, lines 121–122). Speaking as Hamlet, what does the word "nunnery" carry in this line, and what different meanings might the audience have heard in it?

2. Claudius says, "O, 'tis too much" when he hears of the fencing match (not in Act 3), but in Act 3, Scene 1, he admits, "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below. / Words without thoughts never to heaven go" (lines 97–98). Speaking as Claudius, what is he admitting about his own sincerity, and how does the image of words "flying up" shape the way we read his prayer?

3. Hamlet describes the purpose of the play to the Players: "to hold, as 'twere, the mirror up to nature" (Act 3, Scene 2, lines 21–22). Speaking as Hamlet, what does he mean by "nature," and does the play he has chosen hold up that mirror to Claudius, to Gertrude, or to himself?

4. Polonius says, "I'll loose my daughter to him" (Act 2, Scene 2, line 165), but in Act 3, Scene 1, after the nunnery exchange, Claudius asks, "Love? His affections do not that way tend" (line 165). Speaking as Claudius, what does he claim to know about Hamlet's affections, and on what evidence does he base that claim?

5. Hamlet, alone and preparing to kill Claudius, says, "Now might I do it pat, now he is praying" (Act 3, Scene 3, line 73). Speaking as Hamlet, what reason does he give for not acting, and what does the phrase "pat" suggest about his attitude toward the moment?

## Analyze

Focus: how the language builds meaning, tension, and character.

6. Hamlet says to Gertrude, "Nay, but to live / In the rank sweat of an enseamèd bed" (Act 3, Scene 4, lines 92–93). Speaking as Hamlet, why does he choose such physical, corrupt imagery to address his mother, and what does the imagery reveal about how he understands her relationship with Claudius?

7. Hamlet asks, "Is this a dagger which I see before me?" in his soliloquy (Act 2, Scene 1, not Act 3). For Act 3, consider Hamlet's line after he decides not to kill Claudius at prayer: "Up, sword, and know thou a more horrid hent" (Act 3, Scene 3, line 89). Speaking as Hamlet, what is the effect of the sword being personified and given a command, and how does it change our sense of who controls the action?

8. Gertrude responds to the play by asking, "The lady doth protest too much, methinks" (Act 3, Scene 2, line 228). Speaking as Gertrude, is she offering a judgment on the Player Queen, on herself, or on both? Use the word "protest" to support your reading.

9. Claudius, after the play, cries, "Give me some light. Away!" (Act 3, Scene 2, line 268). Speaking as Claudius, what does the demand for light suggest about how he experiences guilt, and how does this moment differ from his earlier composure in Scene 1?

10. Hamlet, speaking to his mother in the closet, says, "Look here, upon this picture, and on this, / The counterfeit presentment of two brothers" (Act 3, Scene 4, lines 53–54). Speaking as Hamlet, what is the function of the word "counterfeit," and how does the comparison between two portraits reshape what the audience is asked to judge?

## Connect and Evaluate

Focus: how the act's language opens questions beyond itself and how strong the reading is.

11. Hamlet tells the Players to "suit the action to the word, the word to the action" (Act 3, Scene 2, lines 17–18). Speaking as Hamlet, how well does Hamlet himself follow this advice in the scenes that follow, and what does his own conduct suggest about the gap between speech and deed?

12. Claudius says, "Pray can I not, / Though inclination be as sharp as will" (Act 3, Scene 3, lines 38–39). Speaking as Claudius, is his failure to pray a failure of faith, of will, or of honesty? Argue which reading the language best supports.

13. Hamlet says, "Conscience does make cowards of us all" (Act 3, Scene 1, line 83). Speaking as Hamlet, is conscience the main obstacle to his revenge, or is something else holding him back? Use at least one other line from Act 3 to test your answer.

14. Ghost, speaking to Hamlet in the closet, says, "Do not forget. This visitation / Is but to whet thy almost blunted purpose" (Act 3, Scene 4, lines 110–111). Speaking as the Ghost, what does the word "blunted" imply about how the Ghost sees Hamlet's progress, and does Hamlet's behavior in the closet confirm or contradict it?

15. Across Act 3, Hamlet repeatedly stages, watches, or speaks about performance. Speaking as Hamlet, does the play-within-a-play give him a clearer view of Claudius's guilt, or does it mainly let him delay acting? Support your position with two specific lines from the act.

## Follow-Up Probes for the Teacher

Use these when discussion stalls or a claim goes unsupported:

- "Which word in that line carries the most weight for you, and why?"
- "Where else in Act 3 does the same word or image appear?"
- "Is that reading the only one the line allows? What would a different reading sound like?"
- "Who in the scene would disagree with that interpretation, and what would they say?"
- "What does the speaker want the listener to do after saying this?"

## Answer Key

The questions are open-ended, so no single answer is correct. Evaluate responses using these criteria:

- Each claim names the speaker and cites the act, scene, and line.
- Quoted words are accurate to the cited line.
- The reading accounts for ambiguity in the language rather than ignoring it.
- The student tests the interpretation against at least one other part of the act.
- Responses build on or respond to peers, not only to the teacher.

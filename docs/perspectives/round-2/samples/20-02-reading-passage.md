# The Long Struggle to Rebuild: Reconstruction, 1865-1877

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Reading Passage

When the Civil War ended in 1865, the United States faced a difficult question. The Union had won, and the Confederate states had lost. But what would the country look like once the fighting stopped? How would the South rejoin the nation? And what would become of the four million people who had been enslaved? The years that followed are known as Reconstruction, and they lasted from 1865 to 1877.

Reconstruction changed the Constitution in three major ways. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States, except as punishment for a crime. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, granted citizenship to all people born or naturalized in the United States, including formerly enslaved people. It also promised "equal protection of the laws" to every citizen. The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, stated that the right to vote could not be denied on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. For the first time, Black men could vote in national elections across the country.

Another important step was the Freedmen's Bureau. Congress created this federal agency in 1865 to help people who had been freed from slavery and poor white families in the South. The Bureau provided food and medical care, set up thousands of schools, and helped people find work and sign labor contracts. It also tried to settle disputes between formerly enslaved people and their former enslavers. The Bureau faced many problems. It had limited money and staff, and many white Southerners opposed its work. Its authority ended in 1872, but its schools and ideas influenced education in the South for years afterward.

During Reconstruction, Black men were elected to state legislatures and to the United States Congress. Their presence in government was a major change. At the same time, opposition in the South was strong. Groups such as the Ku Klux Klan used threats and violence to keep Black citizens from voting and holding office. Some Southern states passed "Black Codes," which limited the rights of freed people, and later laws such as poll taxes and literacy tests made voting harder for Black citizens.

Reconstruction ended in 1877. Several factors contributed. Northern voters grew tired of the cost and conflict involved in keeping federal troops in the South. Economic problems such as the Panic of 1873 drew public attention elsewhere. Many white Northerners also became less willing to enforce civil rights for Black Americans. In 1876 and 1877, a disputed presidential election led to a compromise under which the last federal troops were withdrawn from the South. White Southern Democrats then regained control of state governments. The rights that Reconstruction had promised were not fully enforced for nearly a century.

Historians continue to debate Reconstruction. Some emphasize its achievements, including new constitutional protections and the first large steps toward Black citizenship. Others focus on its failures and the violence that followed. Both views help students understand how the promises of the Amendments and the work of the Freedmen's Bureau were tested by the choices of the people who lived through those years.

# The Workshop

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Reading Passage

1. The workshop smelled of cedar shavings and machine oil, and under those, faintly, of the coffee her father had always left going on the hot plate. Nora stood in the doorway for a long time before she stepped inside. The lease ended in nine days. The landlord had been polite about it, which somehow made it worse. Everything had to go or be carried out to the car, and she had told herself that the practical thing was to start with the easy shelves and work toward the hard ones.

2. The easy shelves held paint cans with their lids hammered flat, jars of brass screws sorted by size in his square capitals, and a row of coffee cans that rattled when she lifted them. None of it was hard to decide about. She filled three boxes and then sat on an overturned bucket and looked at the wall above the bench, where a calendar from a hardware store still showed the month he had died. She had not turned it. She could not have said why.

3. Her father had been a man of few words and fewer gestures. He was not cold, exactly. He came to every recital and every parent-teacher night, and he always asked the right question afterward, the one that showed he had actually listened. But he did not say he loved her, and when she was sixteen and had said something cruel about his stubbornness, he had only nodded and gone back to the garage, and they had not spoken of it again. For years she had read this silence as a verdict. She had decided that he found her hard to love, or at least that he had never worked out how to show her that he did.

4. The hard shelf was at the back, behind the table saw, where the light from the single window did not reach. She had to pull the shelf out from the wall to see what had been pushed behind it. It was a flat tin box, the kind that once held drafting pencils, its corners dented. The lid was not locked. Inside were not tools or receipts but letters, perhaps thirty of them, in envelopes that had been slit cleanly with a knife. The handwriting on the front of each envelope was hers.

5. She did not recognize the first envelope at once. It was postmarked from a city she had lived in during college, and the return address was her own old apartment, and the date was the spring she had been twenty. She opened it carefully. It was a letter she had written him, a short one, about a job she had not gotten and a man she had been too proud to call back. She had forgotten writing it. She had not forgotten the feeling of sending it, the small hope that he would reply with something more than the usual weather report and a mention of the Cardinals.

6. Her hands were not steady as she read the next one, and the one after that. He had kept every letter she had ever sent him from the years she was away, and in the margins of some of them, in pencil so faint she had to tilt the page toward the window, he had written small notes. Beside a line about her roommate: *Good. Be kind to her.* Beside a paragraph about a bad winter in the new city: *Call on Sunday. I will pick up.* Beside a sentence in which she had written, almost as an afterthought, that she was not sure she would ever come home for good: nothing at all, only a single underlined word, which she could not quite make out.

7. She had spent a decade assuming his silences were absences. Now she sat among the sawdust with thirty letters in her lap and understood that she had been reading the wrong document. He had not been silent in these pages. He had been listening, patiently and closely, to a daughter who had never once thought to ask whether he was listening. Whether the notes were tenderness or only a careful man's habit of record, she could not decide, and the more she turned it over, the less the question seemed to matter.

8. The last envelope in the box was not addressed to him at all. It was unsealed, and when she drew out the single sheet, she found it was a draft: her father's handwriting, crossed out in places, beginning *Nora, I have never been good at saying the things*, and then stopping. The rest of the page was blank except for a date, two weeks before his heart had failed in the hospital parking lot, and beneath it, one word, written and then pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly: *Call.*

9. She folded the draft along its old creases and did not put it in any box. She sat with it in her hand while the afternoon light moved off the calendar and onto the floor. Somewhere in the house she had not yet cleaned, the phone that had once hung on the kitchen wall was still plugged in, though no one had dialed a number from it in years. She thought about the Sunday he had said he would pick up. She thought about how many Sundays there had been, and how many of them she had let pass without picking up the phone herself. Then she stood, carried the tin box to the car, and did not go back for the shelf.

## Answer Key

1. Likely reason: she is avoiding the emotionally difficult material and building toward it, or she wants to work through the task gradually. Acceptable answers include avoidance or pacing herself. The passage states she "told herself that the practical thing was to start with the easy shelves."

2. She read his silence as a verdict: she decided he found her hard to love, or that he never learned how to show her he loved her.

3. She found about thirty letters she had written him during her years away, slit neatly and kept in a tin box. Significance: they show he kept and read everything she sent, and he wrote private notes in the margins (for example, "Call on Sunday. I will pick up."), revealing attentiveness she had not perceived.

4. b. light or barely visible. "Faint" describes pencil so light she must tilt the page toward the window to read it.

5. Sample evidence: he kept every letter she sent; he wrote notes such as "Good. Be kind to her." about her roommate and "Call on Sunday. I will pick up." during a bad winter; he asked the right questions at recitals and conferences. Any two well-supported examples are acceptable.

6. The draft reveals that he had wanted to express his feelings directly, beginning "Nora, I have never been good at saying the things," but he did not finish. It shows his difficulty with expression was a known struggle and that he intended to reach out, with the one word "Call" suggesting urgency and regret.

7. The ending leaves the emotional gap unresolved, mirroring real parent-child communication, where understanding often arrives too late or incompletely. Nora's decision to leave the shelf and act on the word "Call" suggests that the responsibility to communicate now passes to her, and the unanswered question about his feelings underscores that silence can carry both love and distance.

8. Sample answer: Nora must reconcile her long-held belief that her father was emotionally distant with the evidence that he had quietly cared for and listened to her all along, while she is also clearing out his workshop after his death.

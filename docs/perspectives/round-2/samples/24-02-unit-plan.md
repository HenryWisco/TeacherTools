# Unit Plan: Of Mice and Men, Close Reading and Literary Argument

**Grade:** 10
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit length:** 3 weeks (15 instructional periods of 50 minutes)
**Class:** Mixed-ability, 28 students
**Text:** *Of Mice and Men* by John Steinbeck (1937), novella

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Unit Overview

Students have not read Steinbeck before. Over three weeks they will read the novella closely in sections, practice analyzing how Steinbeck builds meaning through setting, character, dialogue, symbol and structure, and develop a clear, evidence-based argument about one of the novella's major themes. The unit ends with a literary argument essay of 900 to 1,200 words.

Reading schedule: the novella is divided into six sections (chapters 1 to 6). Teachers should check their edition's chapter breaks and adjust if needed. The reading is assigned in portions alongside class work, so each period has a clear reading target.

Support for a mixed-ability class: provide a vocabulary and dialect glossary (for example, "bindle," "jerkline," "ketch," "crick," "bunkhouse"), offer audio support for students who benefit from it, and use the structured discussion and graphic organizers described below. Advanced students can extend their argument with a second text or critical source.

## Essential Questions

1. What does it mean to belong, and what happens to people who do not?
2. How do dreams give people hope, and how can they also do harm?
3. How does an author use setting, character and structure to make a larger point about society?
4. What responsibility do we have toward people who are vulnerable?

## Learning Goals

**Students will know:**
- The basic plot, characters and setting of *Of Mice and Men*
- Key terms: theme, symbol, foreshadowing, characterization, irony, dramatic irony, tone, and textual evidence
- The historical context of the Great Depression and migrant labor in California's Salinas Valley

**Students will be able to:**
- Read a passage closely and identify how word choice, dialogue and imagery create meaning
- Trace one symbol or motif (such as the dream farm, the rabbits, George's gun, Curley's wife's hair, or the crooked hand) across multiple chapters
- Write a defensible thesis about a theme and support it with at least three pieces of textual evidence with analysis
- Use a quotation correctly with citation (author, page or section)
- Revise a draft using peer feedback and a rubric

## Lesson Plan

| Day | Focus | Main Activity | Check for Understanding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction to Steinbeck and the Depression | Brief context mini-lesson; students complete a K-W-L chart; read the opening description of the Salinas River and the bunkhouse (Ch. 1, first pages) | Exit ticket: name two things the setting tells you about the characters' lives |
| 2 | Characters and first impressions | Read Ch. 1 to the arrival of George and Lennie at the ranch; character map for George and Lennie | Students label one line of dialogue that reveals each character's personality |
| 3 | Close reading skills | Model close reading on the rabbit-dream passage in Ch. 1; students annotate for imagery and repetition | Annotation check: three marked details with one-sentence interpretations |
| 4 | Dialogue and dialect | Read Ch. 2 (ranch bunkhouse, Curley, Curley's wife); discuss how dialogue shows power and loneliness | Quick write: how does Curley's wife's introduction change the mood? |
| 5 | Foreshadowing | Chart foreshadowing in Ch. 1 and 2 (the trouble in the last town, Lennie's dead mice, Curley's hostility) | Students match three foreshadowing clues to what they predict |
| 6 | Reading Ch. 3 and 4 | Jigsaw groups: Crooks's section (Ch. 4) and the Candy section; compare how each man is isolated | Group summary on a shared card; teacher checks for accuracy |
| 7 | Symbol and motif | Trace the "farm" dream and the rabbits across chapters; discuss what the dream represents | Students list three appearances of the dream and what each suggests |
| 8 | Reading Ch. 5 | Read the barn scene; examine Lennie's violence and George's choices; Curley's wife's death | Short response: what does Lennie's reaction reveal about his understanding? |
| 9 | Reading Ch. 6 and the ending | Read the final chapter; discuss the ending and George's decision | Quick poll with written reason: was George's choice an act of love or of despair? |
| 10 | Theme: thesis development | Brainstorm themes (loneliness, the American Dream, power and weakness, friendship); model thesis writing | Students draft one thesis statement; teacher gives feedback on the thesis |
| 11 | Evidence and analysis | Model the "claim, evidence, analysis" paragraph; practice with a sample passage | Students write one body paragraph; peer check using the paragraph checklist |
| 12 | Counterargument and organization | Teach how to address a counterclaim (e.g., George is selfish vs. George is loyal); plan outline | Outline check: thesis, three supporting points, one counterclaim |
| 13 | Drafting | Independent drafting in class; teacher conferences with a sample of students | Draft checkpoint: introduction and first body paragraph |
| 14 | Peer review and revision | Structured peer review using rubric; students revise | Revision reflection: name one change made and why |
| 15 | Final draft, reflection and wrap-up | Final draft due; short reflection on what the novella revealed about the unit's essential questions | Submitted essay and reflection (summative) |

## Formative Assessments

- Exit tickets (Days 1, 2, 4, 9)
- Annotation checks (Day 3)
- Quick writes and polls (Days 4, 9)
- Jigsaw group summaries (Day 6)
- Thesis feedback (Day 10)
- Paragraph peer check (Day 11)
- Outline check (Day 12)
- Draft checkpoint (Day 13)

## Summative Assessment

**Literary argument essay (900 to 1,200 words).** Students write an argument about one theme from *Of Mice and Men*. A reading quiz covering chapters 1 to 6 is given at the end of Week 2 (about 10 questions, short answer and multiple choice).

## Culminating Task: Literary Argument Essay

**Prompt:** Choose one theme from the novella. Write an argument that answers the following question: How does Steinbeck use [your chosen element, such as setting, a symbol, or a character] to develop a theme about [your chosen theme]?

Possible themes: loneliness and isolation; the dream of belonging; power and vulnerability; friendship and its limits; the cost of kindness.

**Requirements:**
- A clear thesis in the introduction
- Three body paragraphs, each with a topic sentence, at least one direct quotation, and analysis that explains how the evidence supports the thesis
- One paragraph that addresses a counterargument
- A conclusion that explains why the theme matters
- Correct MLA-style citation of quotations (author and chapter number, for example: Steinbeck, ch. 3)

**Success Criteria:**

| Criterion | Excellent (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Arguable, specific, previews the reasoning | Arguable and clear | Clear but general or weakly arguable | Missing or unclear |
| Evidence | Three or more well-chosen quotations, accurately cited | Three quotations, mostly well chosen and cited | Quotations present, some weak or miscited | Few or no quotations, or misused |
| Analysis | Explains how each quotation supports the thesis, with insight | Explains most connections clearly | Summarizes more than analyzes | Little or no analysis |
| Counterargument | Fairly represents an opposing view and answers it | Addresses an opposing view | Mentions an opposing view briefly | Missing |
| Organization and style | Logical flow, varied sentences, precise word choice | Clear organization, mostly precise language | Some organization issues; wordiness | Disorganized; frequent errors |
| Conventions | Few or no errors | Minor errors that do not distract | Errors that sometimes distract | Errors that impede meaning |

## Common Misconceptions to Watch For

1. **"George is simply a bad friend for shooting Lennie."** Students often judge the ending too quickly. Ask them to examine what Lennie understands and what George can protect him from.
2. **"Plot summary counts as analysis."** Students retell events instead of explaining how they support the theme. Use the prompt "So what? How does this prove your thesis?" repeatedly.
3. **"The dream farm is just a fantasy that never matters."** Students may miss how the dream shapes characters' hope, behavior and relationships. Trace it across chapters.
4. **"Curley's wife is only a villain or only a victim."** Encourage nuanced readings of her loneliness, her choices and how other characters treat her.
5. **"Dialect and slurs are just background noise."** Students may overlook how language reveals power and prejudice, especially in the treatment of Crooks and the other workers. Discuss this respectfully and address sensitive language directly, with the teacher framing it as part of the novella's historical portrayal.
6. **"Any opinion is an argument."** Students need to understand that an argument requires a claim that can be challenged, supported with evidence and reasoning.
7. **"Quotes can stand alone."** Students often drop quotations without explaining them. Teach the "sandwich" structure: point, quotation, analysis.

## Answer Key

Reading quiz (end of Week 2): answers are provided by the teacher from the edition in use. Key checkpoints include the names and jobs of the main characters, the reason the men must leave Weed, the nature of George and Lennie's dream, the events in the barn in Chapter 5, and the final scene by the Salinas River in Chapter 6. Teachers should check page or section numbers against their edition before distributing.

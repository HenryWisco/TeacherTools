# Front Disc Brake Pad Replacement and Rotor Inspection

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

**Course:** Auto I (CTE Automotive Technology)  **Partners:** ______________________

## Investigation Question

How do you safely lift a vehicle, remove a front disc brake caliper and pads, measure rotor thickness and lateral runout, and decide whether the rotor can be reused or must be replaced?

## Background

Disc brakes stop the vehicle when pads clamp onto a rotor. Pads wear down over time, and rotors can wear unevenly, become thin, or warp, causing pulsation and noise. Manufacturer specifications give a minimum rotor thickness (often stamped on the rotor hub or listed in the service manual) and a maximum allowable lateral runout, usually around 0.001 to 0.002 inch.

## Materials (per pair of students)

- 1 training vehicle with front disc brakes (lifted and supported by instructor-approved jack stands)
- 1 floor jack and 2 jack stands (instructor operates the lift and placement check)
- Lug wrench or impact wrench (instructor-approved)
- Torque wrench (ft-lb scale)
- 3/8-inch drive socket set and combination wrenches
- C-clamp or brake caliper compressor tool
- Wire brush and brake cleaner
- Outside micrometer (0 to 1 inch range)
- Dial indicator with magnetic base
- Shop towels and nitrile gloves
- Brake pad set (correct part number for the vehicle)
- Service manual or specification sheet for the vehicle
- Hub nut or lug nut torque reference chart

## Safety Notes

- Wear safety glasses and nitrile gloves at all times.
- Brake dust may contain harmful particles. Do not blow it off with compressed air. Wipe with a damp rag or use brake cleaner in the shop's ventilated area.
- Never work under a vehicle supported only by a jack. Place jack stands under the frame or designated lift points before any work begins.
- Instructor check required: verifying jack stand placement, lowering the vehicle onto the stands, and final torque verification of lug nuts. Students must not lift or lower the vehicle alone.
- Do not press the brake pedal with the caliper removed. Wheel cylinder or caliper piston may come out of its bore.
- Keep hands clear of the wheel and rotor when spinning the rotor for runout measurement.
- Dispose of used brake pads and cleaning rags in the designated container.

## Procedure

Part A: Preparation and Lift (instructor-supervised)

1. Park the vehicle on level ground, set the parking brake, and chock the rear wheels.
2. Loosen the lug nuts on the front wheel one-half turn while the tire is still on the ground.
3. Using the floor jack at the designated lift point, raise the vehicle until the tire is about 2 inches off the floor.
4. Place a jack stand under the designated support point. Lower the vehicle onto the stand. Instructor checks placement before you continue.
5. Remove the lug nuts and the wheel. Set the wheel flat on the floor.

Part B: Caliper and Pad Removal

6. Locate the caliper guide pin bolts on the back of the caliper. Remove the two bolts with the correct socket.
7. Lift the caliper off the rotor and hang it from the coil spring or hook with a wire. Do not let it hang from the brake hose.
8. Remove the old brake pads and the anti-rattle clips. Note how the pads wear (even or uneven, and the wear indicator tab position).
9. Record pad thickness on the data table. Measure the pad friction material with a ruler or caliper if your shop provides one.

Part C: Rotor Inspection and Measurement

10. Clean the rotor surface with brake cleaner and a shop towel. Wipe off all dust.
11. Visually inspect the rotor for deep grooves, scoring, heat spots, cracks, or a blue discoloration. Record your observations.
12. Measure rotor thickness with the outside micrometer at four points spaced evenly around the friction surface (about 90 degrees apart). Record each reading.
13. Calculate the average rotor thickness and compare it to the minimum thickness stamped on the rotor or listed in the service manual.
14. Mount the magnetic base of the dial indicator on the suspension or steering knuckle so the indicator tip rests on the rotor friction surface about 1/2 inch in from the outer edge.
15. Zero the dial indicator. Slowly rotate the rotor one full turn by hand. Record the highest and lowest readings. Lateral runout equals the difference between them (total indicator reading).
16. Repeat the runout measurement at a second position on the rotor to confirm the result.

Part D: Installation

17. Compress the caliper piston with a C-clamp or caliper tool. Watch the brake fluid level in the master cylinder reservoir so it does not overflow.
18. Install the new pads and anti-rattle clips as they were removed. Apply a thin layer of high-temperature brake grease to the back of the pads and the caliper slide points only where the manual specifies.
19. Reinstall the caliper over the rotor. Install the guide pin bolts and tighten to the specification in the service manual using the torque wrench.
20. Reinstall the wheel and hand-tighten the lug nuts in a star pattern. Lower the vehicle onto its tires (instructor checks). Tighten lug nuts to specification in a star pattern.
21. Pump the brake pedal several times until it feels firm before moving the vehicle. Instructor checks before the vehicle is moved.

## Teacher Notes

**Timing (55 minutes):** Part A about 10 minutes (instructor-led lift and stand check). Part B about 10 minutes. Part C about 15 minutes. Part D about 15 minutes. Analysis and cleanup about 5 minutes. Consider having pairs rotate roles between wheels so each student measures at least once.

**Expected results:** Typical training-vehicle front rotors are roughly 0.9 to 1.1 inch thick when new, with minimum thickness commonly printed on the rotor (often about 0.8 to 0.9 inch). Lateral runout on a good rotor should measure 0.002 inch or less, and many specifications call for 0.001 inch or less. Students should find that a rotor measuring at or near the minimum thickness, or with runout above spec, fails. Verify the specifications for the specific training vehicle before class, since values vary by model.

**Preparation checklist:** Confirm jack stand and lift points on each vehicle. Pre-check torque specifications. Verify that the micrometer is calibrated and the dial indicator reads correctly. Stage brake cleaner, rags, and disposal containers. Have a spare caliper compressor tool available.

**Common student errors:** Forgetting to loosen lug nuts before lifting. Hanging the caliper by the brake hose. Measuring rotor thickness at only one spot. Not zeroing the dial indicator before taking runout readings. Overtightening guide pin bolts.

## Answer Key

1. Answers vary with student measurements. Average = (sum of four readings) divided by 4.
2. If the average is below the stamped minimum, the rotor must be replaced. If it is above the minimum, the rotor may be reused or resurfaced per shop policy and the measured runout.
3. Lateral runout = highest reading minus lowest reading. Example: 0.0015 in minus 0.0005 in gives 0.0010 in.
4. Pulsation or vibration felt in the brake pedal and/or steering wheel during braking, and possible uneven pad wear. Runout causes the pads to contact the rotor unevenly as it turns, pushing the pedal and pulsing the braking force.
5. Rotors can wear unevenly, so one spot may read differently from another. Four readings give a more accurate average and reveal uneven wear.
6. New pads are thicker than worn pads, so the piston must be retracted to make room for the caliper to fit over the rotor and new pads. Pushing the piston back also moves brake fluid back toward the master cylinder.
7. A star pattern pulls the wheel evenly against the hub and prevents warping the rotor or bending the wheel studs.
8. Answers vary. A strong answer cites measured thickness relative to the minimum spec and runout relative to the maximum allowable value, and states which action follows from the data.

# Lesson Plan: Slavery and Its Legacy in American History

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Grade and Subject

Grade 8, Social Studies (U.S. History)
Class length: 50 minutes

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

- I can describe how slavery was organized and enforced in the United States, including its legal basis.
- I can explain how enslaved people resisted and maintained family and community life.
- I can identify at least two ways slavery's legacy affected American life after 1865.
- I can use primary source evidence to support a historical claim.

## Standards

No standards were specified. Align this lesson to your state or district U.S. History standards for the period 1790 to 1877, and to your C3 Framework inquiry practices if your district uses them.

## Materials

- Projector or printed copies of the three primary source excerpts (Source A, Source B, Source C below)
- Timeline strip on the board (see Direct Instruction)
- Exit ticket slips (one per student)
- Pencils and this handout

**Source A (1740 South Carolina Slave Code, excerpt paraphrased for grade level):** "Enslaved people were treated as property. Any enslaved person who ran away could be whipped or maimed, and killing one who resisted capture was not a crime."

**Source B (Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*, 1845, original wording paraphrased):** Douglass described learning to read in secret, because teaching enslaved people to read was illegal in many states, and how reading gave him a way to understand his condition and plan his escape.

**Source C (Excerpt from the 1865 Thirteenth Amendment):** "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| Chattel slavery | A system in which people are owned as property, and their children are born into slavery |
| Slave Code | A law that controlled enslaved people's behavior and punished resistance |
| Resistance | Any action that challenged slavery, from work slowdowns to escape to armed revolt |
| Primary source | A document or object created at the time being studied |
| Legacy | The lasting effects of a past event on later times |
| Sharecropping | After 1865, a system where Black farmers worked land owned by others and paid rent or a share of the crop, often falling into debt |
| Black Codes | Laws passed in Southern states after 1865 that restricted the freedom of Black Americans |
| Jim Crow | State and local laws that enforced racial segregation from the late 1800s to the 1960s |

## Lesson Sequence

### Hook (5 minutes)

Write on the board: **"Freedom is not the same as equality."**

Ask students: "Can you think of a time when you had a right on paper but didn't get to use it fully? What made the difference?" Take three or four responses. Tell students today they will study a period when the law changed, but life did not change at the same speed.

### Direct Instruction (12 minutes)

Draw a timeline on the board with four markers: **1619**, **1787**, **1865**, **1877**.

Explain each point in plain language:

- **1619:** Enslaved Africans arrive in the English colony of Virginia. Over the following two centuries, slavery expands across the colonies and, later, the United States.
- **1787:** The Constitution is written. It counts enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation and taxation, and it protects slavery in several ways.
- **1865:** The Thirteenth Amendment is ratified, ending legal slavery except as punishment for crime.
- **1877:** Federal troops withdraw from the South, ending Reconstruction. Southern states begin passing Black Codes and later Jim Crow laws.

Explain that slavery was a legal system, not just a set of individual cruel acts. Enslaved people were bought and sold, their children inherited their status, and laws (Slave Codes) gave owners broad power and punished enslaved people who resisted.

Then say: "Enslaved people were not passive. They resisted in many ways, including escape, work slowdowns, breaking tools, and preserving languages, music, and family ties."

### Guided Practice (13 minutes)

Project or distribute Source A. Read it aloud, then ask:

1. According to Source A, how were enslaved people legally classified? (Expected answer: as property.)
2. What does this source reveal about what the law expected of enslaved people? (Expected answer: obedience; runaways were punished severely.)
3. What evidence in the source shows the law protected owners' power over enslaved people? (Expected answer: killing a resisting runaway was not a crime.)

Repeat the process with Source B:

4. Why was learning to read dangerous for enslaved people in the 1800s? (Expected answer: literacy gave them access to information and tools for freedom, and laws prohibited teaching it.)
5. What does Douglass's account show about how enslaved people resisted? (Expected answer: they found secret ways to gain knowledge and plan for freedom.)

### Independent Practice (12 minutes)

Students read Source C and answer on their handout.

6. According to Source C, what did the Thirteenth Amendment end? (Expected answer: slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime.)
7. What loophole does the wording leave? (Expected answer: involuntary servitude remains legal as punishment for crime.)

Then students complete this short paragraph, writing 4 to 6 sentences:

8. Explain one way slavery's legacy continued after 1865. Use at least one piece of evidence from Source A, B, or C, or from the timeline.

Sentence starters for students who need them: "One way slavery's legacy continued was ____. Evidence for this is ____. This matters because ____."

### Closure and Exit Ticket (8 minutes)

Briefly share two or three student responses to question 8. Then ask students to complete the exit ticket on a slip of paper or the bottom of the handout.

## Formative Assessment (Exit Ticket)

Students answer these three questions in 1 to 2 sentences each:

1. What legal status did the Thirteenth Amendment give to people formerly held as slaves? Expected answer: They were no longer legally enslaved, though the amendment allowed involuntary servitude as punishment for crime.
2. Name one way enslaved people resisted slavery. Expected answer: Any one of: escape, learning to read in secret, work slowdowns, preserving family ties or culture, armed revolt, legal petitions.
3. Name one legacy of slavery that continued after 1865. Expected answer: Any one of: Black Codes, sharecropping and debt, Jim Crow segregation, unequal access to voting or schooling.

**Success criteria:** Students get at least two of three correct with a complete sentence = on track. Students who give only one correct answer should be re-taught with the timeline and Source A.

## Differentiation

**For students who need support:**
- Provide a word bank with the vocabulary terms and definitions.
- Give the sources with key sentences highlighted, and read them aloud in pairs.
- Offer sentence starters for all written responses.
- Allow oral responses to question 8, recorded by the teacher.

**For students ready for more:**
- Ask: "Why might Southern states have passed Black Codes so quickly after 1865? What did they want to preserve?" (Possible answer: maintaining a labor system and racial hierarchy after the loss of legal slavery.)
- Ask students to compare Source A and Source C: "What changed in the law, and what stayed the same in practice?"
- Have students find one additional primary source online through the Library of Congress or National Archives and explain how it supports or challenges the sources in this lesson.

## Answer Key

1. Enslaved people were treated as property (Source A).
2. The law expected obedience and punished runaways severely.
3. Killing a resisting enslaved person who was trying to escape was not considered a crime.
4. Literacy gave enslaved people knowledge and tools for freedom, and laws made teaching them to read illegal, so reading was a form of resistance.
5. Enslaved people found secret ways to gain knowledge and plan for freedom.
6. The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for crime.
7. Involuntary servitude remains legal as punishment for a crime, which allowed forced labor to continue in some cases.
8. Responses will vary. Accept any reasonable legacy supported by evidence, such as Black Codes, sharecropping, Jim Crow laws, or restrictions on voting and schooling, with a clear link to a source or the timeline.

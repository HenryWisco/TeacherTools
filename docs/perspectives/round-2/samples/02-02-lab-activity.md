# Lab: Finding the Molarity of Acetic Acid in Vinegar by Titration

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

## Investigation Question

How can we determine the molarity and percent by mass of acetic acid in household vinegar using a sodium hydroxide titration?

## Background

Acetic acid (CH3COOH) is a weak acid and the main acid in vinegar. In a titration, a solution of known concentration (the titrant) is added slowly to a measured volume of unknown solution until the reaction is complete. Phenolphthalein is an indicator that stays colorless in acid and turns faint pink at the endpoint, when the acetic acid has just been neutralized by the sodium hydroxide.

The neutralization reaction is 1:1:
CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O

So at the endpoint, moles of acetic acid = moles of NaOH added. Molarity is moles per liter, and percent by mass uses the assumed vinegar density of 1.00 g/mL.

## Materials (per pair)

- Buret (50 mL) and buret clamp with ring stand
- 0.100 M NaOH solution (about 60 mL, labeled, in a squeeze bottle or beaker)
- Household vinegar, about 30 mL (in a labeled beaker)
- 3 Erlenmeyer flasks, 125 mL
- 10.0 mL volumetric pipet with bulb, or a 10 mL graduated pipet
- Phenolphthalein indicator solution (a few drops per trial)
- Distilled water in a wash bottle
- White paper or white tile under the flask
- Funnel (for filling the buret; remove before reading)
- Safety goggles and nitrile gloves for each student
- Paper towels and a waste beaker

## Safety

- Wear chemical splash goggles at all times, including during setup and cleanup.
- Wear nitrile gloves when handling sodium hydroxide. NaOH is caustic and can cause serious eye and skin burns. If it contacts skin, rinse with plenty of water for at least 15 minutes and tell your teacher.
- Vinegar is a dilute acid and is safe to handle, but do not taste or smell any sample directly.
- Do not pipet by mouth. Use a bulb only.
- Read the buret from eye level, with the meniscus at the bottom of the curve.
- Teacher handling: Preparing and pouring the NaOH stock solution, filling burets, and disposing of waste must be supervised by the teacher or another adult. Students should not fill burets from stock bottles.
- Dispose of all solutions in the designated waste container, not down the sink, unless your teacher says otherwise.
- Clean up spills immediately and report them to your teacher.

## Procedure

1. Put on goggles and gloves. Confirm with your partner that the NaOH buret has been filled by the teacher and that the zero mark is at or just below the top.
2. Record the initial buret reading to the nearest 0.01 mL in Data Table 1.
3. Using the volumetric pipet and bulb, transfer exactly 10.00 mL of vinegar into a clean 125 mL Erlenmeyer flask.
4. Add about 25 mL of distilled water to the flask. This makes the endpoint color easier to see and does not change the moles of acid.
5. Add 3 drops of phenolphthalein. The solution should be colorless.
6. Place the flask on a white surface under the buret tip. Swirl the flask continuously with one hand while controlling the buret stopcock with the other.
7. Add NaOH steadily. Near the endpoint, add it drop by drop. The endpoint is reached when one drop produces a faint pink color that persists for at least 30 seconds of swirling.
8. Record the final buret reading to the nearest 0.01 mL. Volume used = final reading minus initial reading.
9. Repeat steps 2 through 8 for Trial 2 and Trial 3. Each trial must use a fresh 10.00 mL portion of vinegar in a clean flask.
10. If two trials agree within 0.20 mL of NaOH used, they are acceptable. Use the average of the acceptable trials in your calculations.
11. Rinse the buret with distilled water, then drain, and dispose of all solutions as directed. Wipe down your bench.

## Calculations

Show all work with units.

**Step A: Moles of NaOH used**
moles NaOH = molarity of NaOH (mol/L) × volume NaOH used (L)

Use the average NaOH volume from Data Table 1.

moles NaOH = ____________ mol

**Step B: Moles of acetic acid in the sample**
Because the reaction is 1:1, moles CH3COOH = moles NaOH.

moles CH3COOH = ____________ mol

**Step C: Molarity of acetic acid in vinegar**
molarity = moles CH3COOH / volume of vinegar (L)

Use 10.00 mL = 0.01000 L.

molarity = ____________ mol/L

**Step D: Mass of acetic acid in the sample**
Molar mass of CH3COOH = 60.05 g/mol

mass CH3COOH = moles CH3COOH × 60.05 g/mol

mass CH3COOH = ____________ g

**Step E: Mass of the vinegar sample**
Mass = volume × density = 10.00 mL × 1.00 g/mL

mass of vinegar = ____________ g

**Step F: Percent by mass of acetic acid**
percent by mass = (mass CH3COOH / mass of vinegar) × 100

percent by mass = ____________ %

## Analysis Questions

1. Why is the reaction between acetic acid and sodium hydroxide described as 1:1? How did this allow you to skip writing a separate mole ratio?
2. Why did you add water to the flask before titrating? Did this change the number of moles of acetic acid? Explain.
3. Your indicator changed color at the endpoint. Why does this color change happen, and why is it a reasonable signal that the acid has been neutralized?
4. Overshooting the endpoint (adding too much NaOH) would make your calculated molarity too high or too low? Explain your reasoning.
5. Compare your percent by mass to the value printed on the vinegar label (usually 5 percent). Calculate the percent error. Suggest two reasons your result might differ from the label.
6. Acetic acid is a weak acid, but this titration still works well. Why does a weak acid titrated with a strong base still give a sharp enough endpoint with phenolphthalein?
7. In a real lab, why would you run at least three trials instead of one?

## Teacher Notes

**Preparation (teacher or lab aide):**
- Prepare 0.100 M NaOH by dissolving 4.00 g of solid NaOH in water and diluting to 1.00 L. Standardize against potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) if time allows. Store in a tightly capped bottle.
- Fill burets with NaOH for each pair. Check for air bubbles in the tip.
- Use household distilled white vinegar, labeled 5 percent acidity. Dispense about 30 mL per pair into labeled beakers.

**Expected results (for 0.100 M NaOH and 10.00 mL vinegar):**
- Volume of NaOH used is typically about 8.3 to 8.9 mL.
- Molarity of acetic acid is about 0.083 to 0.089 mol/L.
- Percent by mass is about 0.50 to 0.53 percent when using 1.00 g/mL density, which is a noticeable mismatch with the label. Note: the label's 5 percent refers to percent by volume, and a 5 percent (w/v) solution would be about 0.83 M. If your class's measured molarity is near 0.85 M, students have correctly identified that the label's 5 percent is by volume and approximately equals 5 g per 100 mL.
- Teachers should check the actual volume they use: for 10.00 mL vinegar with 0.100 M NaOH, the expected NaOH volume is near 8.5 mL when the label is 5 percent.

**Worked example (for checking):**
Suppose average NaOH used = 8.50 mL.
- moles NaOH = 0.100 mol/L × 0.00850 L = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol
- moles CH3COOH = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol
- molarity = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol / 0.01000 L = 0.0850 mol/L
- mass CH3COOH = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol × 60.05 g/mol = 0.0510 g
- mass vinegar = 10.00 mL × 1.00 g/mL = 10.00 g
- percent by mass = (0.0510 / 10.00) × 100 = 0.510 percent

**Common student errors:**
- Reading the buret from the top of the meniscus or at an angle.
- Forgetting to convert mL to L in molarity calculations.
- Using the wrong molar mass or forgetting that the moles of acid equal the moles of base.
- Overtitrating, which shows as a deep pink color that fades slowly.

**Timing (50 minutes):** Setup and safety review, 10 min. Three trials, 25 min. Calculations and analysis, 15 min.

## Answer Key

Calculations (worked example with 8.50 mL NaOH used):
- moles NaOH = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol
- moles CH3COOH = 8.50 × 10^-4 mol
- molarity = 0.0850 mol/L
- mass CH3COOH = 0.0510 g
- mass vinegar = 10.00 g
- percent by mass = 0.510 percent

Analysis questions (sample answers):
1. Each mole of acetic acid reacts with exactly one mole of NaOH, so moles of acid equal moles of base at the endpoint.
2. Adding water changes the volume, not the moles of acetic acid, so it does not change the result. It makes the color change easier to see.
3. Phenolphthalein is colorless in acidic solution and pink in basic solution. The first faint pink shows that the added base has slightly exceeded the acid.
4. Overshooting makes the calculated molarity too high, because more NaOH is counted than was needed.
5. Percent error = |measured − accepted| / accepted × 100. Possible reasons: endpoint overshoot, parallax error in reading the buret, uneven pipetting, or the label being approximate.
6. At the equivalence point of a weak acid with a strong base, the solution is slightly basic, and phenolphthalein changes color in that range (about pH 8.2 to 10), so the endpoint is detected well.
7. Multiple trials reveal random error and show whether results are reproducible, which increases confidence in the average.

# Differentiation Ideas: Causes of the American Revolution Article and Essay

**Name:** ______________________  **Date:** ____________

This sheet is for teacher use. It offers ideas for adapting the three-page article reading and five-paragraph essay so that all 26 students can access the content and show what they know. Note on the test: the lesson is graded with the district writing rubric and counts as the unit test. Any modification that changes the skill being measured (for example, removing the requirement to explain causes) changes the assessment. Check your district's policy and the IEP and 504 plan language before deciding which changes are accommodations (same task, different support) and which are modifications (different task or grading). Several ideas below are marked accordingly.

## Students who need more support

These ideas serve the two students with reading disabilities and any others who struggle to decode or hold a three-page informational text in mind.

1. **Chunk the article into three sections.** Divide the three pages into sections of about one page each, with a short question card for each section, such as "Which British laws made colonists angry?" Students answer on the card before moving on. This is an accommodation.

2. **Provide a text-to-speech version.** Use the district's text-to-speech tool or a read-aloud recording of the article. Students follow along on paper. Check that the audio version contains the same text. This is an accommodation.

3. **Pre-teach five key terms** with a picture or simple definition card: *tax*, *boycott*, *representation*, *Parliament*, *colony*. Post them on the wall and on each desk. This is an accommodation.

4. **Give a cause-and-effect graphic organizer** with three boxes per cause: "What happened," "Why colonists reacted," "What it led to." Students fill in one row per cause before drafting. This is an accommodation.

5. **Offer a shortened reading** with the same main ideas. Highlight or bold the topic sentence of each paragraph in the article. This is an accommodation. If a student's IEP requires a modified text, use it, but keep the causes the same.

6. **Provide a sentence bank** for the essay body: "One important cause was ______ because ______." "This led colonists to ______." "As a result, ______." This is an accommodation.

7. **Allow a conference instead of full independent drafting.** Meet with the student for five minutes to talk through their two causes, then let them dictate or type the essay with a scribe or speech-to-text. This is an accommodation.

8. **Check the reading level of the essay prompt itself.** Rewrite the prompt in plain language at the top of the sheet: "Pick the two causes you think mattered most. Explain why each one made colonists angry enough to fight." This is an accommodation because the task is unchanged.

## English learners

These ideas serve the three English learners, who should be able to show content knowledge even if their academic English is still developing.

1. **Preview the article's vocabulary and background.** Before reading, spend five minutes on a picture walk and a visual timeline of 1763 to 1775, with images of the Stamp Act, the Boston Tea Party, and the Intolerable Acts.

2. **Offer a bilingual glossary** for the five key terms, with Spanish or the students' home language if available. Students may also use a dictionary app that translates.

3. **Let students read the article with a partner** who reads the same text, taking turns by paragraph. Partners may discuss in their home language before writing in English.

4. **Provide the same sentence frames as above**, plus transition words: "First," "Another important cause," "Because of this." This is an accommodation.

5. **Allow the essay in English with a planning phase in any language.** Students may outline both causes in their home language first, then translate the ideas into English sentences. The graded product remains English. This is an accommodation.

6. **Use a visual cause-and-effect chain** on the board with arrows, such as "Stamp Act → colonists protest → Parliament repeals it → Townshend Acts → boycotts." Students copy the chain into their notes.

7. **Check comprehension through speaking first.** Ask each student to explain one cause aloud to you or to a partner before writing. Use this to decide whether they need extra support.

## Students with attention or executive-function needs

These ideas serve the two students with ADHD 504 plans and any others who lose focus during long reading or long writing.

1. **Break the lesson into timed segments** with a visible timer: 15 minutes reading section one, a two-minute movement break, 15 minutes reading section two, and so on. This is an accommodation.

2. **Give a "stop and mark" routine.** After each section, students write one sentence on a sticky note: "The main cause in this section was ______." Sticky notes go on a tracking sheet at the desk. This is an accommodation.

3. **Use a checklist for the essay** with each step visible: choose two causes, fill the organizer, write the introduction with a thesis, write body paragraph 1, write body paragraph 2, write the conclusion, reread once. Students check off each step. This is an accommodation.

4. **Offer a seat near the teacher or a standing workspace** and permission to take a short, scheduled water or movement break. This is an accommodation.

5. **Provide a typed copy or a shorter writing window.** Give the essay in two sessions, with the first session for planning and body paragraphs and the second for introduction, conclusion, and revision. This is an accommodation.

6. **Reduce visual clutter on the article.** Use a clean copy with one column of text and minimal side notes, and place a bookmark or ruler under the current line. This is an accommodation.

## Students ready for more challenge

These ideas extend the task for students who finish early or show strong understanding. None of these should replace the core task or change how the essay is graded.

1. **Ask for a third cause and a comparison.** After writing about two causes, students briefly explain which of the two was more important and why, using evidence from the article. Note: if the rubric scores only two causes, present this as an optional paragraph or a separate reflection so it does not confuse grading.

2. **Consider colonist and British perspectives.** Students write two sentences explaining how a British official might have justified one of the causes. This builds historical thinking without changing the essay.

3. **Connect to a primary source.** Provide a short, public-domain excerpt from a colonial pamphlet or a letter from the period as an optional extension to check against the article. Confirm the source is appropriate and in the public domain before use.

4. **Use precise academic vocabulary.** Challenge students to use words like "grievance," "sovereignty," or "representation" correctly in their essays.

## Three levels of the task

The following versions of the same task use the same content and the same core skill: identifying and explaining two causes of the American Revolution. Only the scaffolding changes. Use the version that matches each student's plan and needs. Check that any version assigned to a student with an IEP is consistent with that IEP.

### Level 1: Full support

Read the article in three sections, using the question card for each section and the key term cards. Complete the cause-and-effect organizer for two causes, choosing from the list of causes on the board. Write a five-paragraph essay using the sentence bank. Each body paragraph starts with a cause from your organizer. Ask for a conference or use speech-to-text if writing is difficult.

### Level 2: Partial support

Read the article, using the key term cards and the topic sentences as guides. Complete the cause-and-effect organizer for two causes you choose. Write a five-paragraph essay. You may use the sentence frames for your introduction and conclusion only.

### Level 3: Independent

Read the article independently. Choose the two causes you think were most important. Explain each cause in a five-paragraph essay with a thesis, two body paragraphs with evidence from the article, and a conclusion that explains which cause mattered more.
